🔢 9.1 Revision of Listing Outcomes
Outcomes are the possible results of an event. A spinner with 8 numbers has 8 outcomes; if we only care about colour there are 3.
💡 An outcome is one “line” in the menu of everything that could happen.
Fundamental Principle of Counting: if one event has m possible outcomes and a second event has n, the two events together have m × n possible outcomes. It extends to any number of events.
💡 A menu with 3 starters and 4 mains: pick a starter, then for each one pick a main – that is 3 lots of 4.
outcomes = m × n (or m × n × p × …)

Book examples: an 8-sector spinner and a coin: 8 × 2 = 16. A boy with 5 shirts, 3 ties and 4 jackets: 5 × 3 × 4 = 60 choices.

🔧 Method: count outcomes of several events
  1. Write the number of choices for each stage.
  2. Multiply the numbers.
  3. Need the actual list? Draw a tree or two-way table.
📘 A school offers 3 modern languages, 2 science subjects and 2 business subjects. Pick one from each set. How many choices?
3 languages, 2 sciences, 2 businesschoices at each stage
3 × 2 × 2 = 12 outcomesmultiply

Two-way tables show the outcomes of two events in an organised grid (rows × columns, so 4 numbers × 3 colours = 12). They are also used to show data with row and column totals. Tree diagrams list every outcome at the end of a branch; the number of branch ends is the number of outcomes.

🔧 Method: complete a two-way table
  1. Find a row or column with only one blank.
  2. Fill it using total − the known numbers.
  3. Repeat until full.
  4. Check: row totals and column totals both add up to the grand total.
⚠️ Common mistake: adding the number of choices instead of multiplying (3 starters and 4 mains is 12 meals, not 7).
📏 9.2 Chance and the Probability Scale
Probability measures how likely an event is, using numbers from 0 to 1. An event that is certain has probability 1; one that cannot happen has probability 0. Every other probability is greater than 0 and less than 1. The more likely an event, the closer its probability is to 1.
💡 It is a fuel gauge: empty (0) means no chance, full (1) means it will definitely happen, half is a 50–50 flip.
WordProbabilityBook example
Impossible0Getting a 7 on a normal die
Even chance½ (50–50)The next person you meet is male
Certain1The sun will rise tomorrow
ProbabilityWhat it means
0The event cannot happen
0.5A “50–50 chance” of happening
75%Occurs three times out of four, i.e. it is likely
1The event will definitely happen
🧩 Remember it: I U E L C – “I Understand Every Last Chance”: Impossible, Unlikely, Even chance, Likely, Certain (0 → 1).
⚠️ Common mistake: a probability can never be bigger than 1 or negative. If you get 5/4 or −0.2, you have gone wrong.
🎯 9.3 Probability and Equally Likely Outcomes
Trial: the act of doing the experiment (throwing a die). The outcomes are the possible results (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). The list of all outcomes is the sample space. The result you want is the event (or favourable outcome): “an even number” is 2, 4, 6.
💡 The sample space is the whole pizza; the event is the slices you want.
Equally likely: two events are equally likely if they have the same chance of happening. For equally likely outcomes the probability of event E is given by:
P(E) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ number of possible outcomes
💡 “How many ways can I win” over “how many ways can anything happen”.
🔧 Method: find P(E)
  1. Count all the possible outcomes (the bottom).
  2. Count the favourable outcomes (the top).
  3. Write top ÷ bottom as a fraction.
  4. Simplify it.
📘 Tiles spell P A R A L L E L O G R A M (13 tiles). One is picked at random. Find (i) P(an A) (ii) P(an O) (iii) P(A or L) (iv) P(A or O)
13 tiles: A × 3, L × 3, O × 1count first
(i) P(A) = 3/133 As of 13
(ii) P(O) = 1/13
(iii) P(A or L) = (3 + 3)/13 = 6/13add the favourable
(iv) P(A or O) = (3 + 1)/13 = 4/13

Spinner with 5 equal sections (1 green, 2 yellow): P(green) = 1/5 and P(yellow) = 2/5.

All probabilities add to 1. Every outcome is covered, so P(E) + P(not E) = 1, i.e. P(not E) = 1 − P(E). (The book’s own reason: “every disc is either even or odd, i.e. every outcome is covered”.)
💡 You are either in the room or not in the room – the two chances fill 100%.
FractionDecimalPercentage

3/50.660%
3/40.7575%

Fraction → decimal: divide top by bottom. Decimal → %: × 100.

⚠️ Common mistake: using “favourable ÷ unfavourable” (3/10 instead of 3/13). The bottom is all the outcomes, including the favourable ones.
🪙 9.4 Two Events – Sample Spaces, Trees and Two-Way Tables
When two events happen (a coin tossed and a die thrown) the sample space is all the pairs: H1, H2 … H6, T1 … T6 – 12 outcomes. Draw it as a tree diagram or a two-way table.
💡 It is a menu: every “first choice” paired with every “second choice”.

Book examples from the 12 outcomes: P(H, 6) = 1/12. P(head and an even number) = {H2, H4, H6} = 3/12 = 1/4.

Two dice have 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes. The grid shows the sum of the two dice:

🔧 Method: probability from a sample-space grid
  1. Draw the grid (or tree) and count the total outcomes (36 for two dice).
  2. Circle the cells that match the event.
  3. Probability = circled ÷ total. Simplify.
📘 Two dice are thrown and the scores added. Find P(total = 7), P(4 or less), P(11 or more), P(multiple of 5).
Total 7: six cells → 6/36the diagonal
P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6
4 or less: 2 (1) + 3 (2) + 4 (3) = 6 → 6/36 = 1/6count each total
11 or more: 11 (2) + 12 (1) = 3 → 3/36 = 1/12
Multiple of 5: 5 (4) + 10 (3) = 7 → 7/36
⚠️ Common mistake: treating the 11 possible totals (2 to 12) as equally likely. They are not – a 7 can happen 6 ways, a 2 only 1 way. Use the 36 outcomes.
🔬 9.5 Estimating Probabilities from Experiments
Real events are not always equally likely, so we run an experiment or survey to estimate the probability. The number of successes ÷ the number of trials is the relative frequency (or experimental probability). It gives an estimate of the true probability.
Relative frequency = number of successful trials ÷ total number of trials
💡 It is a batting average: hits out of goes. After more goes, the average is a better picture of the player.
More trials, better estimate. As the number of trials increases, the relative frequency gets closer to the true (theoretical) probability.

John tossed a coin and recorded heads ÷ tosses: 10 → 0.6, 20 → 0.55, 100 → 0.53, 200 → 0.54, 400 → 0.485, 500 → 0.484, 1000 → 0.49. The value settles close to 0.5, so he concluded the coin was not biased.

📘 Derek counts cars passing the school gate: White 24, Red 32, Black 14, Blue 16, Green 10, Other 4. Find (i) the number surveyed (ii) relative frequency of blue (iii) of red as a decimal (iv) estimate P(next car is green)
(i) 24 + 32 + 14 + 16 + 10 + 4 = 100 carsadd the frequencies
(ii) blue = 16/100 = 4/25
(iii) red = 32/100 = 0.32
(iv) P(green) ≈ 10/100 = 1/10more cars → more reliable
Expected frequency: how many times you expect an event to happen in a number of trials.
expected frequency = probability × number of trials
💡 A bag with 3 blue of 8 discs: on average 3 blue in every 8 picks – so in 400 picks, 3/8 × 400 = 150.
📘 A biased spinner has P(1) = 0.35, P(2) = 0.1, P(3) = 0.25, P(4) = 0.15 and a section 5. (i) Find P(5) (ii) Which number is most likely? (iii) Spun 200 times, how many 3s?
x + 0.35 + 0.1 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 1all add to 1
(i) P(5) = 0.15x + 0.85 = 1
(ii) 1 (0.35 is the biggest)
(iii) 0.25 × 200probability × trials
= 50 threes
🔧 Method: is a die fair?
  1. Work out the expected count: trials ÷ 6.
  2. Compare each face with it.
  3. Close to the expected count everywhere → fair.
  4. One face far away → biased.
⚠️ Common mistake: thinking the expected frequency is exactly what will happen. It is the average over many trials; real results vary (the book: “similar results – yes, identical results – no”).
⭕ 9.6 Probability Using Venn Diagrams
When the information is in a Venn diagram, the probability of an event is the number in the matching region ÷ the total in U (every number in the diagram, including outside the circles).
💡 Think of U as the whole class lined up; the probability is how many in your region of the line.

A ∩ B – both

A ∪ B – A or B

A – all of A

B \ A – B only

(A ∪ B)′ – neither

B′ – not in B

🔧 Method: probability from a Venn diagram
  1. Add every number, including outside, to get the total in U.
  2. Shade the region the question asks for.
  3. Add the numbers in the shaded region.
  4. Region ÷ total; simplify.
📘 A club has 130 members. Football only 40, both games 10, tennis only 35, neither 45. Find the probability a member plays (i) both (ii) tennis but not football (iii) neither (iv) football or tennis
Total = 40 + 10 + 35 + 45 = 130U first
(i) 10/130 = 1/13
(ii) 35/130 = 7/26
(iii) 45/130 = 9/26
(iv) (40 + 10 + 35)/130 = 85/130 = 17/26all of both circles
📘 Three sets: sports played by 25 second-year students (N = netball, B = basketball, S = soccer). Find P(all three), P(netball), P(not soccer), P(two games only).
Total = 6 + 2 + 1 + 4 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 6 = 25check
P(all three) = 1/25the centre
P(netball) = (6 + 2 + 1 + 4)/25 = 13/25whole N circle
P(not soccer) = (6 + 4 + 2 + 6)/25 = 18/25outside the S circle
P(two games only) = (2 + 4 + 1)/25 = 7/25three pair regions
⚠️ Common mistake: dividing by the total in one circle (or leaving out the “neither” number) instead of the total in U. And when asked for “A or B”, count the overlap once.
🌳 9.7 Tree Diagrams – Probability of Multiple Events
A probability tree diagram shows the outcomes of two or more events. The branches show every outcome of each event, and the probability is written on each branch. The probability of an extended branch is found by multiplying the separate probabilities along it.
💡 A route-planner: the chance of the whole journey is the chance of leg 1 and leg 2 together – so multiply.

Three children: each is a boy or a girl with probability ½, giving 8 outcomes, each with probability ½ × ½ × ½ = 1/8. P(3 boys) = P(B and B and B) = 1/8. The three families with one girl are BBG, BGB and GBB, so P(one girl) = P(BBG or BGB or GBB) = 1/8 + 1/8 + 1/8 = 3/8.

🧩 Remember it: Along the branches ×, between the branches +. “And” (one path) → multiply. “Or” (more than one path) → add.
📘 Spinner A: red 1/4, blue 3/4. Spinner B: red 3/5, blue 2/5. Both are spun. Find (i) P(A red, B red) (ii) P(both the same colour)
(i) P(A red, B red) = 1/4 × 3/5 = 3/20multiply along
(ii) Same colour = (A red, B red) or (A blue, B blue)two paths
= 3/20 + 6/20 = 9/20add the paths
Check: 3/20 + 2/20 + 9/20 + 6/20 = 1branch ends add to 1
🔧 Method: tree-diagram probability
  1. Draw a branch for each outcome of the first event, then of the second.
  2. Write the probability on every branch (those leaving one point add to 1).
  3. Find the path(s) the question wants.
  4. Multiply along each path; add the paths.
  5. Check all the branch ends add to 1.

Trees are especially useful when the events do not have an equal chance of happening, as in the spinner example.

⚠️ Common mistake: adding along a branch instead of multiplying, or forgetting a second path (for “one of each” there are two: red-then-blue and blue-then-red).

❓ Quick Quiz

An “early-bird” menu has 3 starters and 4 main courses. How many different 2-course meals can you have?

  1. 7
  2. 12
  3. 34
  4. 81
Fundamental Principle of Counting: 3 × 4 = 12. 7 adds the choices instead of multiplying; 81 is 34; 34 just writes the numbers side by side.

An event has probability 3/10. Which is the same value?

  1. 0.03
  2. 0.3
  3. 3%
  4. 0.7
3/10 = 3 ÷ 10 = 0.3 = 30%. 0.03 puts the decimal point in the wrong place; 3% is only 3/100; 0.7 is 1 − 0.3 = P(not the event).

An event has a probability of 75%. What does this mean?

  1. It cannot happen
  2. It has a 50–50 chance
  3. It happens three times out of four, so it is likely
  4. It will definitely happen
75% = 3/4: three times out of four. 0 means it cannot happen, 0.5 is a 50–50 chance, and 1 means certain.

One of the 13 tiles P A R A L L E L O G R A M is picked at random. What is P(an L)?

  1. 1/13
  2. 3/13
  3. 3/10
  4. 10/13
There are 3 Ls among 13 tiles: 3/13. 1/13 counted just one L; 3/10 divides by the number of tiles that are not L; 10/13 is P(not L).

For the same 13 tiles, what is P(an A or an O)?

  1. 4/13
  2. 3/13
  3. 2/13
  4. 3/169
3 As + 1 O = 4 favourable out of 13: 4/13. 3/13 forgets the O; 2/13 comes from 4/26 (adding the totals as well); 3/169 multiplies, which is for “and” on a tree, not “or”.

A die is thrown. Order these from least likely to most likely: (a) the number is even, (b) the number is larger than 4, (c) the number is less than 6.

  1. (b), (a), (c)
  2. (a), (b), (c)
  3. (c), (a), (b)
  4. (a), (c), (b)
P(b) = 2/6 = 1/3 (5, 6); P(a) = 3/6 = 1/2; P(c) = 5/6. So b is least likely, then a, then c. Option 3 is the order from most likely.

A box holds 12 green, 6 blue and 8 white marbles. A marble is picked at random. What is P(not green)?

  1. 6/13
  2. 7/13
  3. 10/13
  4. 4/13
26 marbles; not green = 6 + 8 = 14, so 14/26 = 7/13 (or 1 − 6/13). 6/13 is P(green); 10/13 is P(green or white); 4/13 is P(white).

Two dice are thrown and the scores added. What is P(a total of 9)?

  1. 1/36
  2. 1/9
  3. 1/4
  4. 1/11
A 9 can be (3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3): 4 of the 36 outcomes, so 4/36 = 1/9. 1/36 counts just one way; 1/4 is 9/36; 1/11 pretends the 11 totals are equally likely.

50 cars pass the school gate and 15 of them are red. What is the estimated probability that the next car is red?

  1. 3/7
  2. 3/10
  3. 7/10
  4. 1/15
Relative frequency = 15/50 = 3/10. 3/7 divides red by the 35 non-red cars; 7/10 is P(not red).

Jane tests a drawing pin 20 times (10 “point up”). Nicky tests it 100 times (75 “point up”). Whose result is likely to be more reliable?

  1. Jane’s, because 1/2 is a simpler fraction
  2. They are equally reliable
  3. Nicky’s, because he did more trials
  4. Neither, relative frequency is never reliable
The more trials, the closer the relative frequency is to the true probability. 100 trials beat 20.

In a street of 82 houses, 14 have only a cat, 12 have a cat and a dog, 26 have only a dog and 30 have neither. A house is picked at random. What is P(a cat or a dog)?

  1. 26/41
  2. 32/41
  3. 20/41
  4. 15/41
Cat or dog = 14 + 12 + 26 = 52 out of 82 = 26/41. 32/41 counts the 12 twice (64/82); 20/41 is only one pet (40/82); 15/41 is neither (30/82).

Bag A has 2 red beads and 1 white bead. Bag B has 1 red bead and 2 white beads. One bead is taken from each bag. What is P(both red)?

  1. 1
  2. 1/3
  3. 4/9
  4. 2/9
Multiply along the branch: 2/3 × 1/3 = 2/9. 1 comes from adding 2/3 + 1/3; 4/9 is P(both the same colour) = 2/9 + 2/9; 1/3 is just bag B’s red.

14. (Ex 9.7 Q3) Paula has a die with 5 red faces and 1 green face. She rolls it twice. Use a tree diagram to find the probability that the die shows the same colour each time.
P(red) = 5/6 · P(green) = 1/6 on each rolllabel the branches
P(red, red) = 5/6 × 5/6 = 25/36multiply along
P(green, green) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36
Same colour = 25/36 + 1/36 = 26/36 = 13/18add the two paths
