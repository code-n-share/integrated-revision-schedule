🌐 7.1 — Reasons for European Exploration

In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, many European countries began to explore the wider world by sea. New technology made long voyages possible for the first time, beginning a period of several hundred years when Europeans first explored, then took over, much of the rest of the world.

Key Timeline

1487
Bartolomeu Dias rounds the southern tip of Africa (Cape of Good Hope)

Problems faced by sailors in the early 1400s
  • Sea travel was limited and difficult — sailors could not navigate accurately
  • Sailors were afraid of the unknown; some believed the earth was flat
  • Others feared sea monsters lurked in distant waters
Six Reasons Europeans began to explore (from mid-1400s)
  1. Influence of the Renaissance: People were eager to learn and question old beliefs. The rediscovery of Geographia by the Roman writer Ptolemy changed how Europeans understood the world.
  2. Travels of Marco Polo: His accounts of China's great wealth made people want to experience these wonders themselves.
  3. New trade routes: After the Black Death, Europe's population grew and became wealthier. The spice trade was very profitable; explorers who found new routes to the East could get rich.
  4. Fall of Constantinople (1453): The Great Silk Road was cut off when the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople. New routes between Europe and Asia were urgently needed.
  5. Desire for empire: European rulers sponsored voyages to expand their territory. Atlantic-coast countries (Portugal, Spain, France, England) wanted to bypass Italy's hold on Mediterranean trade.
  6. Spreading Christianity: The Pope encouraged Christian rulers to defeat Muslims. Explorers often aimed to convert peoples they discovered.
📖 Did You Know?

Legends told of a mythical Christian kingdom in Africa or Asia ruled by a king named Prester John. If found, he would unite with Christendom to defeat Islam — another motivation for exploration.

⚙️ 7.2 — Technological Change: Advances in Navigation

Before the new technology, European sailors stayed close to coastlines. For ships to sail straight out into open ocean without a single landmark, better navigation was needed.

New Maps

New, more accurate maps were needed. Cartographers (people who draw maps) used maps brought from Constantinople that were far more detailed. The Portuguese developed portolan charts, which mapped harbours and coastlines more precisely, recording currents, tides and depth. As explorers returned, they updated these maps.

New Equipment
📐
Quadrant & Astrolabe
Helped determine a ship's latitude (distance from the equator) by measuring the position of the stars and the sun.
🧭
Compass
Used to identify north, so the direction the ship was sailing could always be calculated.
🪢
Log and Line
Measured a ship's speed in knots. A sailor threw the log overboard and counted how many knots passed through his hand in a given time (measured by hourglass). Recorded in the logbook.
⚖️
Line and Lead Weight
Used to measure the depth of water, especially near coasts, to ensure the sea was not too shallow for the ship.
New Ships — The Caravel

The fifteenth century saw a revolutionary new ship design: the caravel. It was large and sturdy enough for long voyages and able to sail in all winds, combining the best of Atlantic and Mediterranean ship designs.

⛵
Triangular Lateen Sails
Allowed ships to sail into the wind and made them easier to manoeuvre in bays and along coasts.
🪵
Carvel-built Hulls
Planks fitted edge to edge (unlike Viking overlapping planks) — far lighter. Ships could be built bigger with more masts and carry more men and supplies.
🚢
Rudder
Made the caravel much easier to steer accurately at sea.
🏰
Castle
A structure at the back of the deck that improved crew quarters and served as a defensible area in case of attack.
Life on Board Ship
  • Life during a voyage of discovery was extremely tough
  • The navigator had his own cabin; most sailors slept on deck or below deck in hammocks
  • Constant hard work: raising sails, steering, cleaning, repairing, preparing food
  • Mutiny (sailors rebelling against their captain) was not unusual; men were flogged or chained for breaking rules
  • Food was scarce — sailors often ate flat hard bread (ship's biscuit) made from water, flour and salt
  • Typhoid fever (from lack of fresh water) and scurvy (lack of vitamin C / citrus fruit) were common and could cause death
💡
Exam Tip — Navigation Equipment Learn all seven: Portolan charts · Quadrant · Astrolabe · Compass · Log and line · Line and lead weight · Caravels (with lateen sails and carvel-built hulls)
🗺️ 7.3 — The Voyages of Exploration
The Early Portuguese Voyages

The first major voyages of discovery set out from Portugal in the 1400s. Portugal was ideally placed — on the edge of Europe and close to Africa. Its kings wanted new trade routes to protect Portugal from its more powerful neighbour, Spain.

Prince Henry the Navigator set up a school of navigation at Sagres, bringing together cartographers, instrument-makers, astronomers and experienced sailors. The Portuguese focused on exploring the African coast to find a sea route from Africa to Asia.

  • Over the following decades they discovered the Azores, the Canary Islands and the Cape Verde Islands, and rounded the Gold Coast (modern-day Sierra Leone)
  • In 1487, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa — now called the Cape of Good Hope
  • In 1497, Vasco da Gama continued up the eastern African coast and sailed east from modern-day Kenya to Calicut in India. The round trip took two years
Results of the Portuguese Voyages
  • Made Portugal enormously wealthy
  • Portugal established a large empire in Africa, Asia and later South America
  • Gained control of the spice trade by defeating Arab and Muslim kingdoms
  • Imports of spices into Europe increased dramatically
  • Other European rulers were inspired to launch their own voyages of discovery
The Discovery of the 'New World'

As Portugal controlled trade routes around Africa, its rival Spain had to look west across the Atlantic Ocean for opportunities to discover new lands.

🧑‍✈️
Christopher Columbus
1451–1506 · Change Maker

Born in Genoa, Italy in 1451. A merchant sailor who studied at Sagres. Columbus was convinced the quickest way to China was by sailing west across the Atlantic. He thought the world was significantly smaller than it actually is.

After many European rulers rejected his plan, he convinced King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain. They gave him three ships: the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa Maria.

On 12 October 1492 — 69 days after leaving Spain — land was sighted. Columbus named it San Salvador and called the inhabitants Indians, believing he had reached India. He returned to Spain with slaves, exotic fruit, maize, parrots and gold. People believed he had found a sea route to Asia and he was appointed governor of the new territory.

🔄 Reinterpreting the Past

Columbus did not prove the Earth was round — most educated people in the 1400s already knew it was. This myth was invented by nineteenth-century US historians. Greek scholars like Pythagoras and Aristotle had determined the planet was a sphere over 2,000 years before Columbus.

The Age of Exploration After Columbus
  • John Cabot landed in Newfoundland, Canada, and claimed it for English king Henry VII
  • Ferdinand Magellan led a fleet on a voyage to finally prove the world was round by circumnavigating (sailing around) the globe
  • Abel Tasman was the first European to find Tasmania, south of Australia, and New Zealand
⚔️ 7.4 — The Conquest of the New World

Rumours of entire cities made of gold spread quickly around Europe. Men known as conquistadores (Spanish for 'conquerors') travelled across the sea to conquer indigenous American civilisations, such as the Aztecs and the Incas.

🍫 Did You Know?

The cacao bean — from which we get chocolate — was sacred to the Aztecs, used only by the elite. The Aztecs' drink was bitter, heavily spiced and quite intoxicating. The Spanish brought beans home but didn't enjoy the taste; our chocolate is the result of their experiments with milk and sugar. The word 'chocolate' comes from the Aztec word 'xocoatl'.

Conquering the Aztecs

The Aztecs came from the south of modern-day Mexico. By 1519 their empire covered central Mexico and contained around 489 cities. Their capital, Tenochtitlan, was a city of over 250,000 people on an island in the middle of a lake — Mexico City stands on the same spot today.

  • The Aztecs had a complex society with a king and priests at the top
  • No iron or steel — most weapons were made from wood, stone or copper
  • No horses, cattle, sheep, pigs or goats before Europeans arrived
  • They worshipped many gods; the most important was the sun god, Huitzilopochtli — they believed he needed to be nourished by human blood sacrifice
🏴‍☠️
Hernán Cortés
Spanish Conquistador · Conquered the Aztecs 1519–1521

Hearing of the Aztecs' vast riches, Cortés left the Spanish colonies in the Caribbean in February 1519 with 500 soldiers and 13 horses. He burned his ships to show there was no going back.

He made alliances with local tribes conquered by the Aztecs. Many Aztecs were terrified of his horses and cannons. His key translator was a woman called Malinche who spoke multiple languages.

Cortés marched to Tenochtitlan, where the Aztec ruler Montezuma greeted him — the Aztecs believed he was the returning god Quetzalcoatl. The Spaniards soon began to steal and were driven out. Cortés regrouped and returned with 100,000 men, besieged Tenochtitlan for three months, and the city fell. Its people were massacred. Cortés built his capital on the site of Tenochtitlan.

Conquering the Incas

To the south of the Aztecs lay an even more powerful civilisation. The Incas controlled vast territory in the Andes mountains along the western coast of South America. Their empire was ruled from the capital Cuzco by a god-king.

  • Sophisticated engineers, architects and very fine artists
  • Used no written script, but kept records using a system of knotted string
  • Roads ran to every corner of their empire; they collected tribute from their subjects
🗡️
Francisco Pizarro
1475–1541 · Conquered the Incas 1532

A Spanish soldier with a reputation for ruthless behaviour. He heard of the great wealth of the Incas and was granted a commission to conquer them by King Charles V of Spain in 1528. He gathered just 180 men and 27 horses and invaded in 1532.

At Cajamarca, Pizarro met the Incan king Atahualpa. He had a priest approach with a Bible; when the king threw it aside, Pizarro attacked. Although vastly outnumbered, Spanish superior weapons won the day. The Incas offered to fill a room with gold and silver for their king's release — Pizarro accepted the treasure but executed Atahualpa anyway.

The death of Atahualpa threw the empire into chaos. Pizarro defeated the remaining armies at Cuzco, declared the Spanish province of New Castile and built a new capital at Lima. Huge deposits of gold found in the Andes made Spain the wealthiest country in Europe.

🌏 7.5 — The Impact of Colonisation

Colonisation is when a country takes over another territory and settles some of its own people there to control it. In the century after Columbus arrived in the Americas, about 300,000 people from Spain and Portugal settled in Latin America.

Impact on the New World — The Indigenous Population
🦠 Disease & Population Collapse
  • In 1519, South America's indigenous population was roughly 25 million
  • By 1605, it had fallen to about 3 million
  • Cocoliztli (causing fevers and bleeding), smallpox, measles and influenza were introduced by Europeans who had built up resistance
  • Indigenous peoples had no immune defences — almost 90% of the indigenous population died as a result
🏛️ Destruction of Cultures
  • The sharp decline in indigenous population contributed to the loss of civilisations
  • As Spanish and Portuguese became languages of the new states, indigenous languages and customs disappeared
  • The Catholic Church set up in the New World; Jesuits carried out hundreds of thousands of conversions
  • Those who continued worshipping old gods were severely punished
The Slavery Triangle

Because indigenous peoples could not be taken as slaves and were dying from disease, millions of Africans were transported across the Atlantic. The slavery triangle was a trade triangle whereby:

  • Europe → Americas: European-manufactured goods (cloth, glass, iron, guns)
  • Africa → Americas: Enslaved Africans transported to work plantations and mines
  • Americas → Europe: Raw materials (sugar, cotton, tobacco, rum) sent back to Europe
Impact on Europe
  1. Other European states — England, France, the Netherlands — saw Spain and Portugal's wealth and created their own empires.
  2. So many countries were competing for territory that conflict was inevitable. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the New World between Spain and Portugal.
  3. Huge deposits of gold and silver from the Andes made Spain the most powerful country in Europe throughout the 1500s and 1600s.
  4. The Columbian exchange: the exchange of foods, animals and technologies between Europe and the Americas changed both continents forever. Horses, cattle, sheep, new farming methods and technologies like steel went to the Americas; potatoes, chocolate, coffee, tomatoes and tobacco came to Europe.
🔑 Key Terms
Caravel
A type of ship large and sturdy enough to make long voyages and sail in all winds; featured lateen sails and carvel-built hulls.
Conquistadores
Spanish for 'conquerors'; soldiers who conquered indigenous American civilisations such as the Aztecs and the Incas.
Colonisation
When a country takes over another territory and settles some of its own people there to control it.
Slavery Triangle
The trade triangle whereby enslaved Africans were delivered to the Americas; American raw materials to Europe; and European-manufactured goods back to Africa.
Columbian Exchange
The exchange of foods, animals and technologies between Europe and the Americas that changed both continents forever.
Portolan Chart
A detailed nautical map developed by the Portuguese that recorded harbours, coastlines, currents, tides and depth.
Mutiny
When sailors rebel against their captain. Was not unusual on long voyages; punishments were severe (flogging, chains).
Cocoliztli
An epidemic disease characterised by high fevers and bleeding, introduced by Europeans; one of the main causes of the catastrophic collapse of indigenous populations.
❓ Quick Quiz — Reveal Answers
Name four reasons why Europeans began to explore from the mid-1400s.
Reasons
Any four of: (1) Influence of the Renaissance — people eager to learn. (2) Travels of Marco Polo — stories of Eastern wealth. (3) New trade routes — the spice trade was very profitable. (4) Fall of Constantinople (1453) — the Silk Road was cut off, new routes needed. (5) Desire for empire — rulers wanted to expand territory. (6) Spreading Christianity — the Pope encouraged converting peoples they discovered.
What was the caravel and what were its key features?
Technology
A new type of ship first developed in the fifteenth century. It was large and sturdy enough for long ocean voyages and able to sail in all winds. Key features: triangular lateen sails (could sail into the wind), carvel-built hulls (lighter and bigger), rudders (easier to steer), and a castle at the back for crew quarters and defence.
What did a portolan chart show sailors?
Technology
Portolan charts were detailed nautical maps that showed harbours, coastlines, currents, tides and depth. They were far more accurate than older maps and were updated after each voyage of discovery.
What did Prince Henry the Navigator do to support exploration?
Voyages
Prince Henry set up a school of navigation at Sagres in Portugal. It brought together cartographers, instrument-makers, astronomers and experienced sailors. The Portuguese then focused on exploring the African coast to find a sea route from Europe to Asia.
What voyages did Bartolomeu Dias and Vasco da Gama make?
Voyages
Bartolomeu Dias (1487): Rounded the southern tip of Africa, now called the Cape of Good Hope. Vasco da Gama (1497): Continued up the eastern African coast and sailed east to Calicut in India. The round trip took two years, opening a sea route from Europe to Asia.
Why did Columbus call the people he met 'Indians'?
Columbus
Because Columbus was convinced he had reached India. He thought he had sailed west across the Atlantic and arrived in Asia. He did not realise he had arrived in an entirely different continent — the Americas.
Why were the Aztecs defeated by Cortés despite vastly outnumbering him?
Conquest
Several reasons: (1) Cortés made alliances with local tribes who hated the Aztecs. (2) Many Aztecs were terrified of his horses and cannons (unknown to them). (3) The Aztecs initially believed Cortés was the returning god Quetzalcoatl. (4) The Spanish had superior iron and steel weapons, armour and military tactics.
What was the Columbian exchange?
Impact
The exchange of foods, animals and technologies between Europe and the Americas that changed both continents forever. To the Americas: horses, cattle, sheep, new farming methods, steel technology. To Europe: potatoes, chocolate, coffee, tomatoes, tobacco.
Explain the slavery triangle.
Impact
A three-way trade route: Europe → Americas: manufactured goods (cloth, glass, iron, guns). Africa → Americas: enslaved Africans transported to replace the dying indigenous workforce on sugar, tobacco and cotton plantations and in gold and silver mines. Americas → Europe: raw materials (sugar, cotton, tobacco, rum).
How did European diseases affect the indigenous population of South America?
Impact
In 1519, South America's indigenous population was roughly 25 million. By 1605 it had fallen to about 3 million. Europeans brought diseases like smallpox, Cocoliztli, measles and influenza. Europeans had built up resistance over time, but indigenous peoples had no immune defences, so almost 90% of the indigenous population died as a result.
What was the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) and why was it needed?
Impact
A treaty negotiated by the Pope that divided the New World between Spain and Portugal. It was needed because so many European countries were competing for new territories that conflict between Spain and Portugal was becoming inevitable.
