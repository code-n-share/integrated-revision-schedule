📜 The Historian Chapter 1
1 What is History?
History
The study of the past.
Sources
Provide evidence about the past.
Prehistory
The period of time before writing was used to record history.
Archaeologist
Studies things left behind by people from the past, like artefacts.
Artefact
Any human-made object from the past.
Archaeology
The study of remains from the past.
2 Why We Study History
  • To learn how people from the past lived
  • To understand the causes and consequences of past events
  • To understand how human experience has shaped our world today
  • To learn to recognise patterns of change
3 How We Study History
  • Historians cross-check more than one source to make sure information is correct when investigating and studying the past
  • Historical consciousness — being able to place ourselves in past human experience and link the past, present and future
  • Archives — catalogue and store written sources
  • Museums — collect and display sources and objects for the public
4 Sources

Primary Source

A first-hand account that comes directly from the time being studied.

Secondary Source

A second-hand account that comes from after the time being studied.

Types of Sources
Tactile, visual, aural, oral, written
Census
An official survey of the population.
Biography
An account of someone's life written by someone else.
Autobiography
An account of someone's life written by the person themselves.
5 Reliability — Key Terms
Accuracy
Is the information right/correct?
Bias
Unfairly favouring one side over the other.
Exaggeration
Making something sound/appear better or worse than it is.
Propaganda
Information designed to influence the behaviours/attitudes of the public.
Reinterpretation
Viewing something in a new or different light.
6 Timelines
AD
'Anno Domini' — 'the Year of our Lord'. The period of time after Christ was born.
BC
'Before Christ'. The period of time before Christ was born. Numbers count down toward 1.
Chronology
Means the order in which events took place.
⛏️ The Archaeologist Chapter 2
1 What is Archaeology?
  • It is the study of remains from the past — the only source of information we have about prehistoric times
  • Archaeologists work with historians to study and date these remains to learn about the past
  • An excavation is when archaeologists dig up the ground to find evidence from the past
2 How Archaeologists Find Sites
  • By examining old buildings/ruins
  • Aerial photographs — photos taken from a drone or helicopter that might reveal patterns in the soil suggesting something buried underneath
  • Research archaeology — old documents or records might reveal information about old historical sites
  • Rescue archaeology — making sure that no historical evidence is destroyed before building new structures
  • Accidental discovery of sites/artefacts (e.g. the Ardagh Chalice)
3 The Job of an Archaeologist
  • Carry out a survey and dig test trenches to see if the site is worth excavating
  • Remove topsoil with a digger
  • Dig carefully with trowels and shovels to recover small, delicate items
  • Use brushes and sieves to recover small delicate items
  • Record the location of anything found in the excavation's site book
  • Put recovered artefacts in labelled bags and send them to the laboratory for testing and dating
4 Methods Used in Archaeology
Radiocarbon / Carbon-14 Dating
Measures the amount of carbon-14 left in an organism. Determines how old an organism is.
Geophysical Survey
Like an X-ray of the ground — maps/images are made of underground evidence without an excavation.
Pollen Analysis
The study of pollen remains to determine what was growing at a site during a particular period.
Stratigraphy
Dating artefacts/evidence by how deeply they are buried in the soil. Deeper = older.
Dendrochronology / Tree-Ring Dating
Measures how old a tree is by the number of rings in the trunk/branch.
DNA Testing
Can reveal a person's origins or ethnicity.
3D Reconstruction
Can reveal what someone looked like while they were still alive.
Examining Bones
Can tell us a lot about a deceased person and their lifestyle.
Conservation
When artefacts are protected and preserved so they do not decay.
🏰 The Normans & Introduction to the Middle Ages Middle Ages
1 Introduction — The Middle Ages
  • The Middle Ages (or medieval times) refers to the period of European history from around 500–1,500 AD. Warfare and violence were widespread and constant.
  • While the Vikings were settling elsewhere too (like north-west France), this area became known as Normandy and the people who lived there as Normans.
  • In 1066, the King of England died without an heir and William, the Duke of Normandy, claimed the English crown. When this claim was rejected by the English nobles, William invaded England and won the Battle of Hastings. He became King of England and the story of the Norman Conquest is told in the Bayeux Tapestry.
  • Feudalism was the system of land ownership where rulers divided land among their followers in return for loyalty and taxes. Social hierarchy: king → vassals → knights → peasants.
2 Life in a Norman Castle
  • William the Conqueror divided his land among his followers (vassals or nobles). While taking control of new land, a lord would build a temporary motte and bailey — a small artificial hill (motte) with a wooden tower or keep on top, surrounded by fences and a ditch or moat.
  • Below was an enclosed area where soldiers lived (bailey).
  • Once control of land was secured, the lord would build a permanent stone castle to live in.
  • Stone castles had these key parts: curtain walls, a keep, a great hall, a chapel, dungeons, battlements, towers/turrets, a bailey, the lord's apartments, a gatehouse, a drawbridge, a portcullis and a moat.
  • Castles were key to the defence of a lord's lands and the whole kingdom. Defenders of the castle would fire arrows and pour boiling water or oil on invaders using catapults, battering rams or siege towers.
3 Lords and Ladies
  • A Norman lady was usually married at a young age. Marriages were often arranged to seal an alliance. A dowry was a sum of money or land paid to the groom.
  • The lady's main duty was to produce heirs, teach the children and run the household. The lord's main duty was to maintain order, act as a judge and train soldiers.
  • Sometimes he went hunting or held tournaments for knights. Feasts were held in the great hall and musicians and jesters provided entertainment.
4 Soldiers in the Middle Ages
  • The Norman army was made up of three types of soldiers: foot soldiers, archers and knights.
  • There were three stages to becoming a knight: At age 7, the page was fostered by another family and learned to ride a horse, use a sword and have good manners.
  • At age 14, the squire (learning to fight on horseback and would accompany the lord into battle).
  • At age of 21, he spent the night before the dubbing ceremony in prayer. He swore the oath of chivalry before the lord touched his shoulders with a sword and gave him a manor to manage.
5 Life in the Countryside
  • Most people in the Middle Ages lived in the countryside and lived in villages called manors owned by a knight or lord. Each manor had a manor house, a church, a mill, a blacksmith's forge, a house for the bailiff, and woodland for the lord to hunt and the villagers to collect wood.
  • Farmers had an open field system of farming — fields were divided into sections and one section was left fallow (empty) each year to let the soil regain its nutrients.
  • There were two kinds of peasant: Freemen and serfs. Freemen had to pay a title to the lord. A serf could not leave the manor or marry without permission.
  • Serfs worked six days a week and received a small plot of land in return. If a serf escaped a stayed free for a year and a day, he became a freeman. Their main diet consisted of bread, cheese and pottage. They drank ale. They grew vegetables and kept a few animals.
6 Life in a Medieval Town
  • Norman towns were much smaller than towns today — many were built on rivers or near the coast. Each town needed a charter from the king.
  • Each town was different, but most had: high walls, gates, a main or high street, a market square, narrow streets, a church or cathedral and a fair green.
  • The average person's house was made of wood; only important buildings were made of stone. Houses had no toilets — people used chamber pots which were emptied into the streets. Fire was a constant danger so towns imposed curfews where people had to extinguish their fires at a certain time. Life was generally very cramped and dirty and disease spread easily.
  • Craftsmen produced the everyday goods that towns needed — they had guilds to set standards for their work, set prices/wages and care for sick/old members. Boys usually learned their father's trade. At age 12 he became an apprentice. At age 18 he became a journeyman. If they decided it was good enough he created a masterpiece for a guild.
7 Religion in the Middle Ages
  • Medieval Europe (including Ireland) was almost entirely Catholic. Europe was constantly in fear of going to hell because religion was so important.
  • The Pope was the head of the Catholic Church and the most powerful ruler in Europe. Europe was divided into dioceses run by archbishops and bishops, and then into parishes run by priests.
  • Kings gave land and money to the Church in the hope this would earn them a place in heaven, and the Church became extremely wealthy as a result.
  • There were two different styles of cathedral built in the Middle Ages: Gothic and Romanesque.
  • Priests, unlike most peasants, were able to read and write so priests were often asked to write letters or offer legal advice. They said Mass in Latin every Sunday and collected tithes from parishioners.
  • Monasteries were the first places to offer education to children based on the ability rather than their parents' wealth. A boy who wanted to become a monk would join a monastery as a novice around the age of 13 or younger.
  • The head of a medieval monastery was called an abbot (an abbess for a convent). Monks took three vows: chastity, poverty and obedience. The crown of the head was shaved (the tonsure). After years, if the abbot agreed he took his vows of language of the Church (learned to read and write in Latin). Some monks preferred to live among people to help them better — these monks were called friars.
8 Health and Medicine in the Middle Ages
  • Medieval medicine was based on Ancient Greek writings. Treatments included cupping, leeching and amputation. For illnesses, monks used Monasteries and Herbal medicine.
  • Medieval conditions were cramped and filthy with no sanitation or basic hygiene. People were poor and did not have a good diet. People were less able to fight off infection, became infected, and people were so weak that even minor wounds could be fatal.
  • The worst diseases to affect Europe in the Middle Ages included: smallpox and influenza. Many women and infants also died in childbirth. Many Ages included typhoid, leprosy, smallpox and influenza.
  • One third of Europe's population was killed by the bubonic plague or 'Black Death'. It was extremely contagious and spread quickly. Bodies were simply left on the street to be collected and dumped in mass graves or burial pits. People had no idea what caused the plague so sought to cure it through human sin, fasting or whipping themselves.
  • Doctors wore masks filled with dried flowers and herbs to repel infection. Europe was greatly changed by the Black Death — as many serfs left manors and moved to towns to demand better treatment from their lords.
9 The Normans in Ireland
  • In 1167, the King of Leinster, Dermot McMurrough, was stripped of his kingdom by the King of Ireland, Rory O'Connor. McMurrough made a deal with a Norman nobleman named Richard de Clare (nicknamed Strongbow) that he could marry McMurrough's daughter and become King of Leinster if he helped McMurrough regain his lands.
  • In 1169, McMurrough invaded Ireland with a Norman army and easily defeated the Vikings. Strongbow became King of Leinster after McMurrough's death.
  • In 1171, Strongbow could become too powerful, so the English King Henry II landed in Ireland and combined their forces in 1170, taking Dublin city and ending Viking rule in Dublin, ending the Viking trail.
  • Impact on Ireland — The Normans never fully conquered Ireland. The Norman names became very common in Ireland. The Gaelic Brehon law was replaced by English common law. Norman names became very common in Ireland. These Anglo-Irish families adopted Irish Gaelic law and customs. The Normans intermarried — between Gaelic and Norman families leading to new Anglo-Irish families. Gaelic law and customs were dressed Gaelic and followed Gaelic law.
  • Ireland — English rule limited to the Pale.
10 Legacy of the Normans
  • When William the Conqueror became King of England in 1066, he established a new ruling dynasty. All kings and queens of England since then are directly descended from him.
  • The Normans made significant contributions to architecture across England and Ireland — building stone castles and Romanesque and Gothic churches.
  • After 1066, French became the language of government in England. As a result, the development of the English language was hugely influenced by French. As English nobility spoke French, it replaced the native Gaelic Irish (from France).
  • The Normans did not just conquer England — they were considered the best warriors in all of medieval Europe. Their most important military involvements were the Crusades — a series of invasions between 1095 and 1291 by European Christians to try to seize control of the Holy Land from its Muslim rulers.
🎨 The Renaissance Renaissance
1 Introduction
  • The word Renaissance means 'rebirth'. It refers to the rebirth of European interest in Ancient Greek and Roman civilisations.
  • Humanism was very popular during the Renaissance — a shift in mindset from a focus on God to a focus on humans and human knowledge. People began to question old medieval ideas and develop new ideas in art, science, architecture, literature and medicine.
  • This was very different to the Middle Ages, where everything was seen in the context of Church teachings.
2 Why did the Renaissance Begin in Italy?
  • Italy was at the centre of the Roman Empire — remains of Roman art/buildings were everywhere. The Italian language was based on Latin which made it easier for Italians to read ancient works.
  • When Constantinople was captured by the Turks in 1453, many Greek scholars fled to Italy and brought ancient Greek and Roman manuscripts with them.
  • Italy had grown rich from trade — merchants had lots of money to spend on works of art/architecture.
  • Italy was made up of independent city states all in competition with each other over territory, wealth and and in advanced civilisations like China, India and Arabia.
  • There were many patrons (wealthy people who paid artists to produce art for them) during the Renaissance including the Catholic Church and the de Medici family.
3 Changes in Painting

Medieval Paintings

  • Mainly religious images — to encourage people to follow church teachings
  • Two-dimensional
  • People's bodies not to scale
  • Identical faces with lifeless expressions
  • Painted on wooden panels or onto wet plaster (fresco)
  • Egg tempera paint — egg yolk mixed with powdered pigment

Renaissance Paintings

  • Much more varied subject matter
  • More realistic; three-dimensional, perspective
  • Study of anatomy and use of real-life models
  • Faces looked more lifelike
  • Painted on canvas or wet plaster (fresco)
  • Paint mixed with oil so dried slowly. Sfumato — smoky effect. More nudity — influence of humanism
🖼️
Mona Lisa (Leonardo Da Vinci) One of the world's most famous paintings, housed in the Louvre Museum in Paris. It is a portrait of the wife of a wealthy Florentine merchant and took two years to complete. Use of light and shadow, sfumato, detail on face and body.

The Last Supper (Leonardo Da Vinci) Shows the moment when Jesus tells his disciples that one of them will betray him. Use of perspective and depth. Da Vinci experimented with oil and tempera onto dry wall; the paint soon began peeling off the wall — many attempts have been made to preserve it.
4 Changes in Sculpture

Medieval Sculptures

  • Not very realistic
  • Bodies long and thin
  • Not in proportion
  • Used to decorate churches/monuments rather than free-standing
  • Long straight clothing, not much detail

Renaissance Sculptures

  • More realistic
  • Greater attention to detail
  • Used real life models and studied anatomy to make bodies as realistic as possible
  • Many sculptures tended to be naked, like Ancient Greek sculptures
🗿
Michelangelo's David — one of the most famous Renaissance sculptures. It depicts the biblical figure David (from the story of David and Goliath) with a slingshot on his shoulder. It represents the ideal human body. Michelangelo was born near Florence and studied in Lorenzo de Medici's sculpture academy. As a child he depicted great interest in sculpting and studied in Michelangelo's sculpture academy. His works include the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, which he painted by lying on scaffolding over four years, and the Pietà (a sculpture of Mary holding the body of the crucified Jesus). He was also an architect who designed the St Peter's Basilica.
5 The Printing Press + Literature
  • The Renaissance spread from Italy to the rest of Europe thanks to artists visiting Italy and learning from Italian masters, outside trade with Italy spreading ideas around Europe.
  • Before the 1400s, all books in Europe had to be copied by hand so there were very few in circulation and very few people knew how to read and write.
  • Johannes Gutenberg, a German goldsmith, invented the movable type printing press. The first book he printed was the Bible. Printed books were cheaper than handwritten manuscripts so 3/4 of these to learn off by heart.
  • More people learned to read and write. People read more and were introduced to new ideas. Fiction became popular as people began to read for entertainment.
  • The use of Latin declined as writers wrote in the vernacular (their own language). The Catholic Church had less control over learning and ideas declined.
  • William Shakespeare is considered one of the greatest ever playwrights in English language. He was born in Stratford-upon-Avon, England and joined a company of actors called The King's Men. He quickly became the most popular and famous playwright in England and the company opened a theatre called the Globe. He wrote a total of 38 plays.
6 Health and Medicine (Renaissance)
  • Ideas about medicine began to change as doctors studied anatomy.
  • Andreas Vesalius wrote Oh the Fabric of the Human Body which was full of correct information and drawings.
  • William Harvey discovered that the heart pumped blood around the body.
  • Medical treatments improved considerably now that they were based on accurate information.
7 Science (Renaissance)
  • Since ancient times, people believed the Earth was the centre of the solar system — the Catholic Church held this view as it supported the belief that God created the Earth and mankind. This was challenged by astronomers.
  • Nicholas Copernicus wrote a book in which he claimed that the Earth rotated around the Sun. Galileo read it and later backed up his findings.
  • Galileo Galilei improved on the Dutch invention of the telescope, and discovered that the moon has craters and mountains and that Saturn has rings. The Catholic Church remained very powerful during the Renaissance and opposed any scientific enquiries that went against their teachings — they banned doctors from examining bodies and Copernicus didn't publish his book until he knew he was dying, fearing their reaction.
  • When Galileo revived his ideas, he was tried and convicted of heresy by the Inquisition. To avoid being burned at the stake, he agreed to say that he was wrong and remained under house arrest until his death.
🧭 The Age of Exploration Exploration
1 Reasons for Exploration
  • At first, sea travel was difficult — many sailors were afraid of the unknown (sea monsters, flat earth etc.) and ships couldn't navigate properly.
  • Influence of the Renaissance: people were questioning their existing beliefs and were eager to learn.
  • The stories of Marco Polo, an Italian explorer who travelled to China, wrote about the great wealth and incredible things he had seen — people wanted to see these things themselves.
  • In 1453, the Great Silk Road (main overland trade route for spices) was cut off when Constantinople fell, so new trade routes between Europe and Asia were needed.
  • European rulers wanted to expand their territory and increase their power. The Pope encouraged Christian rulers to spread Christianity.
2 Advances in Navigation Technology
  • Cartographers (mapmakers) started using more detailed maps from Constantinople. The Portuguese developed portolan charts which mapped harbours and coastlines more precisely. As explorers returned from voyages, the information they brought was used to update maps.
  • A quadrant and astrolabe helped determine a ship's latitude (distance from the equator).
  • A compass was used to identify the direction north.
  • A log and line measured a ship's speed in knots. A line and lead was used to measure the depth of the water beneath the ship's logbook.
  • New ships called caravels were designed — large and sturdy enough to make long voyages with triangular lateen sails, carvel-built hulls and rudders for steerage.
  • Life on board a ship was extremely tough with constant hard work. The captain had his own cabin but most of the sailors slept on the deck. Mutiny (sailors rebelling against their captain) was common. Harsh discipline and flogging (whipping) were used to keep order. Ill health was also common: typhoid fever and scurvy.
3 Portuguese Voyages of Exploration
  • The first major Portuguese voyages of discovery set out from Portugal in the 1400s. Prince Henry the Navigator set up a school of navigation in Sagres.
  • The Portuguese focused on exploring the coast of Africa to try to find a way around Africa to Asia. Over the following decades they discovered the Cape Verde Islands, the Canary Islands and set up bases along the modern-day Sierra-Leone and African coast.
  • In 1487, Bartholomew Diaz sailed down the coast of Africa and rounded the southern tip of Africa which was named the Cape of Good Hope, in the hope that it could be used to find a route to Asia.
  • In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed past the Cape of Good Hope and up the eastern African coast, then sailed from modern-day Kenya to Calicut in India. The round trip took two years. Da Gama had proven that it was possible to sail around Africa and reach Asia and later China. Trade made Portugal wealthy. Portugal gained control of the spice trade. Portugal established a large empire in Africa and Asia.
4 Christopher Columbus
  • The most famous explorer of the Atlantic was Christopher Columbus, who reached the Americas in 1492. He was born in Italy and studied at Sagres navigation school.
  • He convinced King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain to sponsor a voyage across the Atlantic to try to reach India and China. They gave him three ships: the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa Maria. Land was sighted 69 days after they left Spain.
  • Columbus called it 'San Salvador' as he thought they had reached India. He called the inhabitants 'Indians'. He returned to Spain with some indigenous slaves, exotic fruit, parrots, maize and a little gold and was appointed governor of the new territory.
  • Spanish settlers travelled to the islands. After Columbus's successful voyage, Spanish settlers realised that Columbus had not reached India but a whole new continent. They enslaved indigenous peoples and forced them to work in mines or on sugar plantations.
  • By the mid-1490s, the indigenous population had been almost entirely wiped out by disease and enslavement. The Spanish began to import African slaves to replace the indigenous population.
  • Explorers continued to explore the world over the next 200 years. John Cabot landed in Newfoundland and Ferdinand Magellan circumnavigated the world.
5 The Conquest of the Aztecs and Incas
  • Stories of the New World inspired Spanish conquistadors to conquer indigenous American civilisations like the Aztecs and Incas.
  • The Aztecs had a complex society very different from European societies. Their capital city of Tenochtitlán (where Mexico City stands today) was a large, advanced city. The Aztecs worshipped many gods, including the sun god, and performed human sacrifices.
  • In 1519, Hernán Cortés landed with 11 ships and made contact with local tribes that had been conquered by the Aztecs who marched to Tenochtitlán where they were greeted by Aztec king Montezuma. The Aztecs mistook Cortés for one of their gods. Spaniards later captured Montezuma after protests over stealing gold. Montezuma was killed and Cortés later returned with 100,000 men to Tenochtitlán. The city fell in Cuzco by a god-king. The Incan empire was ruled from its capital in Cuzco. Skilled engineers, architects and artists. In 1528, Francisco Pizarro was granted permission by the King of Spain to conquer the Incas.
  • The Incas were a powerful empire in South America. Francisco Pizarro defeated them — he defeated the remaining Incas and had their leader Atahualpa executed. Pizarro took the treasure and silver. He established a new capital city in Lima.
6 The Impact of Colonisation
  • The indigenous population was decimated due to violence, mistreatment and disease — they had no resistance to diseases like smallpox, influenza and measles that were introduced by the Europeans.
  • The destruction of cultures: indigenous languages and customs disappeared. Priests set up Christian churches in the New World and carried out thousands of conversions, but privately many indigenous people continued to worship their many gods.
  • Massive growth in the slave trade which led to the 'slavery triangle' — African slaves were shipped to the Americas, European manufactured goods to Africa, American raw materials to Europe and the Americas.
  • So many countries were scrambling for territory that conflict was inevitable. The Treaty of Tordesillas was signed in 1494 which divided the New World between Spain and Portugal. Huge deposits of gold and silver were shipped back to Spain, making it the most powerful country in Europe throughout the 1500s and 1600s.
  • The 'Columbian exchange' changed both continents forever: horses, cattle, sheep, new farming methods and technologies were introduced to the Americas and potatoes, chillies, avocadoes, cocoa coffee and tobacco were introduced to Europe.
