📖 1.1 What is History?
History
The study of the past. The word comes from the Greek word historia, meaning knowledge from investigation.

History is based on evidence and the study of sources. It concerns the story of human activity — from the lives of ordinary people (their jobs, clothing, entertainment) to major events, investigating not only what happened but why.

Source
Something that gives us information or evidence about a person, place or thing in the past.
💡 Sources are the raw material of history. No source = no history!
History vs Prehistory
✍️
History
Everything that has happened since people began using writing. Written sources and documents can be used as evidence. Example: the Annals of the Four Masters.
🪨
Prehistory
The period of time before writing was used. We rely on archaeology for evidence. Example: a weapon made to hunt food is a prehistoric source.
⛏️
Archaeology
The study of the remains left by people in the past. Archaeologists investigate places and objects, including the time before written records were kept.
You will learn more about archaeology in Chapter 2.
🎓 1.2 Why We Study History

Through the study of history, we can:

👀
Learn how people lived
Understand life before us — how people worked, dressed, ate and spent their time.
⚡
Understand causes & effects
Gain understanding of why past events happened and what their consequences were.
🌍
Shape the present
Understand how human experience has shaped our world today, and avoid repeating past mistakes.
🔄
Recognise patterns
Learn to recognise patterns of change across different periods of time.
Historical Consciousness
Being able to place ourselves in past human experience, linking the past, the present and the future.
💡 George Santayana: "Those who do not remember the past are condemned to repeat it."
Who Studies History?
📚
Historian
Someone who is an expert in, or a student of, history. Historians gather evidence from a variety of sources to piece together information about the past.
🏺
Archaeologist
Someone who investigates places and objects left by people in the past, including the time before written records were kept.
🔍 1.3 The Job of the Historian
Finding Evidence

The historian gathers as much evidence as possible from different sources, then cross-checks their work using multiple sources.

Cross-Checking
When more than one source is used to make sure the information is correct.
Historical Repository
A place where evidence of the past can be stored and maintained — for example, artefacts, government documents, manuscripts or photographs.

Historians visit repositories such as libraries, archives and museums to find evidence and sources.

📁
Archive
A place that catalogues and stores a collection of written and other sources. Example: the National Archives of Ireland in Dublin, where records of the Irish State are held.
🏛️
Museum
A place that collects and displays objects for public education and appreciation. Example: the National Museum of Ireland — home to Bronze Age metalwork, Viking artefacts and ancient Egyptian treasures.
💡
The Broighter Boat (c.100 BC) In the National Museum of Ireland, artefacts like the Broighter Boat add to our knowledge of the Iron Age, showing how skilled the Celts were with metalwork and the type of boats used in trade with Britain and western Europe.

📄 Sources Used by Historians

Sources can be split into two categories: primary and secondary.

1️⃣
Primary Source
A source from the time in question; a first-hand account of what happened. Created by someone who witnessed or participated in the event.
★ Generally tells us more clearly what life was like at the time.
2️⃣
Secondary Source
A source from a later date, after the time in question. Created by someone who did not witness the event directly.
★ Gives background information that helps us understand primary sources.

Useful Definitions
🏺
Artefact
Any human-made object, e.g. pottery, a tool or a weapon such as a spear.
📊
Census
An official survey of a population. In Ireland, a census is held every five years.
✍️
Autobiography
An account of a person's life written by the person themselves.
📕
Biography
An account of a person's life written by someone else.
📦
Handling Box
A box containing replicas (copies) of artefacts, so that anyone can examine them without damaging an original.
⚖️ 1.4 Working with Sources: Reliability

Historians must carefully assess how reliable a source is. They examine the point of view of the person behind the source, being aware of:

✔️
Accuracy
Judging how correct the information you are using is.
⚖️
Bias
When an account is not balanced, but unfairly favours one side. Can be deliberate or unconscious. Historians must watch for bias in sources — and in themselves!
📣
Exaggeration
When something is represented as better or worse than it actually was.
📢
Propaganda
Information designed to influence the attitudes and behaviours of the general public. Often biased, appeals to emotions (fear, anger, loyalty) and may even be made up. Common in wartime.

⚠️
Primary vs Secondary — Strengths & Limits Primary sources tell us more clearly what life was like at the time, but can be biased. Secondary sources give useful background context, but we must check how they were created and whether the author was reliable.
📅 1.5 Time and Timelines
Chronology
Putting events into the sequence in which they happened. This makes it easier to follow the story of what happened.

Historians use time in several ways:

  • Divide time into hours, days, weeks, months and years.
  • Group years into decades (10 years), centuries (100 years) and millennia (1,000 years).
  • Organise events using a common feature from a period — e.g. the Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age.
  • Use timelines to show the order in which events happened.
BCE / BC and CE / AD

In the Western world, the birth of Jesus Christ is the fixed starting point. Dates go either forwards or backwards from this point.

⬅️
BCE / BC dates
Before the Common Era / Before Christ. Example: the first Olympic Games took place in 776 BCE (776 years before Jesus was born). Bigger number = earlier! Think of it as a countdown.
776 BC → 700 BC → 1 → CE begins. Bigger BC number = further back in time.
➡️
CE / AD dates
Common Era / Anno Domini ('Year of Our Lord' in Latin). Example: 2003 CE was 2,003 years after the birth of Jesus — just over two millennia.
The 21st century = AD 2000–2099. Both BCE/CE and BC/AD sets mean the same thing.
⚠️
Centuries can be confusing! The year 776 BC was in the 8th century BC: it began with 799 BC and ended with 700 BC. The smaller number is actually later in time for BC dates. AD 1066 is in the 11th century (1000–1099).
🔄 1.6 Reinterpreting History
Reinterpretation
To see something in a new or different light. When a new piece of evidence emerges, it may lead to an event or time in history being reinterpreted.
📘 Real Example — Women in the War of Independence
For decades after the Irish War of Independence, the role of women was not widely recognised or celebrated. However, recent research uncovering evidence in military archives and pension files has shown the crucial roles played by women in the struggle for independence — providing safe houses, distributing money, obtaining and smuggling arms, visiting prisons, and spying. This new evidence led to a reinterpretation of this period of Irish history.
💡
History is never fully finished! New evidence can always emerge — from archives, archaeology or technology — that forces us to reconsider what we thought we knew. This is why historians must keep an open mind.
