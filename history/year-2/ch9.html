🗺️ 1. Ireland in the 1500s

Ireland was divided into three major groups:

  • People loyal to the English Crown who lived in the Pale (Dublin and its surrounding areas, the only part of Ireland under English control).
  • The Anglo-Irish – descended from the Anglo-Normans, they had become independent of the English crown and had adopted Gaelic laws and traditions.
  • The Gaelic Irish chieftains, who followed Brehon Law.
🧩 Remember it: Three groups: Pale (loyal), Anglo-Irish (went Gaelic), Gaelic (Brehon Law).
👑 2. The Tudors
  • The first Tudor monarch was King Henry VII.
  • His son Henry VIII was originally Catholic, but broke from the church to divorce his first wife Catherine of Aragon as she had not provided him with a male heir.
  • He then married Anne Boleyn and set up his own church, the Church of England, with himself as head (Act of Supremacy).
  • Henry had a total of six wives and three children: Mary (by his first wife), Elizabeth (by Anne Boleyn) and Edward (by his third wife, Jane Seymour).
⚠️ Common mistake: Henry VII was the first Tudor; Henry VIII broke from the Church. Don’t swap them.
🎯 3. The Tudors’ Reasons for the Plantations
  • To expand their territory beyond the Pale
  • To spread English laws, customs and culture
  • To spread Protestantism
  • To prevent the Catholic Gaelic Irish forming alliances with other Catholic countries to attack England
  • To prevent further rebellions from the Gaelic Irish
  • To save money
Before the plantations: At first, Henry attempted to control Ireland using peaceful means through a policy of surrender and regrant.
🧩 Remember it: L-L-R-P-A-M hook: Land, Laws/culture, Rebellion stopped, Protestantism, Alliances stopped, Money saved.
1️⃣ 4. First Plantation – Laois-Offaly (Mary I)
  • The O’Moores and the O’Connors of Laois and Offaly frequently raided the Pale.
  • Mary I ordered their lands to be confiscated as punishment.
  • It was a failure: not enough planters came from England, the confiscated lands were still attacked by the Gaelic Irish, and English customs/laws didn’t spread.
  • Gaelic planters had to be given land.
  • Led to better planning for future plantations.
2️⃣ 5. Second Plantation – Munster (Elizabeth I)
  • Munster was ruled by the Fitzgeralds of Desmond, who remained Catholic.
  • Elizabeth I, who was Protestant, sent adventurers to claim land and spread English law, language and religion.
  • Partially successful: new trades, towns and farming methods were set up, but only a small number of planters came (about 4,000; the Crown wanted 20,000) and confiscated lands were still attacked by the Gaelic Irish.
  • Planters still had to rent to the Gaelic Irish.
  • Lessons were learned for future plantations.
3️⃣ 6. Final Plantation – Ulster (James I) ★
Most important: This is the plantation you need to know best.
  • Ulster was dominated by powerful Gaelic Irish clans like the O’Neills of Tyrone and the O’Donnells of Donegal.
  • Organised by James I. The land was divided into smaller estates than Munster and the rules for planters (undertakers, servitors and loyal Irish) were stricter.
  • James reserved the county of Derry for craft guilds.
  • More successful than both previous plantations: many planters came (large numbers of English and Scottish settlers), the Protestant population increased, new towns and markets were set up, English-style houses and castles were built and the Irish were driven off the land that had always been theirs.
  • Cromwell confiscated more land.
  • Rebellions persisted. Tensions flared between Catholics and Protestants and still exist to this day.
🧩 Remember it: Ulster = Undertakers, Large numbers of settlers, Smaller estates, Towns, English-style houses, Rebellions kept going.
⚠️ Common mistake: Don’t say the earlier plantations succeeded – Laois-Offaly was a failure, Munster only partially successful. Ulster was the most successful.
🪪 7. Effect of the Plantations on Irish Identity
  • Most of Ireland’s population remained Catholic, but by 1700 Protestants owned 85% of the land.
  • This wealthy landlord class became known as the Protestant Ascendancy.
  • The division and inequality between Catholics and Protestants gave rise to a long history of anger, distrust and violence.
  • Catholics were also discriminated against by the Penal Laws.
  • Gaelic Irish laws, language, culture and farming methods declined.
🏘️ 8. Growth of Towns
  • Many Irish towns founded as Viking settlements (wattle and daub houses) continued to grow during and because of the plantations.
  • Dublin is an example of a medieval town: the centre of Norman rule in Ireland and a busy port for foreign trade. Many worked at a trade/craft, but it was very unsanitary.
  • Plantation towns like Killarney and Portlaoise had a different layout (courthouse, gaol and Protestant church).
  • Derry was renamed Londonderry by the London guilds who worked there.
  • After the plantations, landowners built market towns close to their estates, which expanded as canals and railways better connected the country. Transport links such as Mullingar and coast towns like Ballina were founded.
  • Ireland’s larger cities like Dublin and Belfast also expanded.
