The Renaissance was a hugely important period in European history. 'Renaissance' means rebirth — specifically the rebirth of European interest in the civilisations of Ancient Greece and Rome. During this period, people began to question old ideas from the Middle Ages and develop new ideas about art, architecture, science, literature and medicine.

Renaissance
The rebirth of European interest in the civilisations of Ancient Greece and Rome. During this period, people developed new ideas about art, architecture, science, literature and medicine.
💡 The word comes from French/Italian meaning 'rebirth'.
Humanism
A shift in mindset from a focus on God to a focus on human knowledge. Artists focused on recreating the real world, nature and real people in their work — very different from the Middle Ages when everything was seen through the lens of Church teaching.
🏛️ 6.1 The Causes of the Renaissance

Although the Renaissance would eventually affect all of Europe, it began in Italy. There are several reasons for this:

🏟️
Remains of the Roman Empire
Italy was the centre of the ancient Roman Empire. Roman buildings, art and statues were everywhere. The Italian language was based on Latin, making it easier to read ancient works. This inspired Italians to copy their ancestors' achievements.
📚
Fall of Constantinople (1453)
Constantinople, capital of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire, was captured by the Ottoman Turks. Many Greek scholars fled to Italy, bringing priceless ancient Greek and Roman manuscripts — books from the ancient world that Italians had never seen.
⚖️
Wealth from Trade
Italy's position in the centre of the Mediterranean meant it had grown wealthy from trade in silks and spices from the East. Merchants had money to spend on art and architecture to show their wealth and power.
🧮
New Ideas from Trade
Traders brought back ideas as well as goods. Merchants returned from Turkey, India and Arabia with new ideas from advanced civilisations — for example, they brought the Arabic number system (1, 2, 3…) which we still use today.
🏙️
Competition Between City States
Italy was not one country but many independent city states competing over territory, trade, wealth and power. To show who was richest and most powerful, they all hired artists and built great buildings. Florence was among the most important.
Patron
A wealthy person who commissioned (hired) an artist to produce a work of art for them. There were many types of patrons: the Catholic Church, kings and governments, and private individuals such as the de Medici family.
🌆 Florence: A Renaissance City

By 1400, Florence was the wealthiest city in Italy, with a population of over 100,000. Its wealth came initially from the wool trade and later from banking. Florentines were very proud of their city and willing to spend lavishly on works of art to decorate it.

Florence was a republic, but most of the power in the city lay in the hands of the de Medici family. They owned the largest bank in the city and used their money to ensure that the men who led the city did what they wanted. They were very important patrons, especially Cosimo (1389–1464) and his grandson Lorenzo (1449–1492). They sponsored artists and architects, set up schools for the study of Roman and Greek manuscripts, and established an academy in their own palace.

💡
Exam Tip — The de Medici Family The de Medici are a key example of Renaissance patrons. Remember Cosimo and Lorenzo de Medici. They used their banking wealth to fund artists and scholars, making Florence the centre of the Renaissance.
🎨 6.2 Changes in Art
Medieval Painting vs Renaissance Painting
Medieval Art
  • Mainly religious images (Bible, life of Jesus)
  • Painted on wooden panels or wet plaster (fresco)
  • Egg tempera — dried quickly, limited colours
  • No real depth — images appeared two-dimensional
  • Bodies not to scale; limbs wrong size
  • Faces the same with lifeless expressions
  • Jesus depicted with an adult face as a child
Renaissance Art
  • Varied subjects: mythology, portraits, landscapes, everyday life as well as religious images
  • Painted on canvas or wet plaster; paint mixed with oil
  • Oil paint dried slowly — more flexible, varied colours
  • Sfumato — blurring lines to create a smoky, lifelike effect
  • Perspective — creating depth and distance
  • Artists studied anatomy closely
  • Real-life people used as models; wrinkles, muscles, shadows depicted
Sfumato
Meaning 'smoky'. A technique of blurring or softening lines and colours to soften textures and create a smoky effect, making paintings more lifelike.
Perspective
The creation of depth and distance in a painting. Objects and people appear smaller in the background and larger in the foreground, creating a three-dimensional effect.
Anatomy
The study of the structure of the human body. Renaissance artists studied anatomy closely to make people in their paintings and sculptures look realistic.
🤔 Did You Know?

During the Renaissance, purple paint was very rare and expensive, as the pigment was only produced from shells in a small area of the Mediterranean. Artists had to use special techniques like glazing — layering red and blue paint — to achieve the colour.

🖼️ 6.3 Key Renaissance Paintings
🖌️ Artist Profile
Leonardo da Vinci, 1452–1519

Da Vinci was one of the greatest geniuses of the Renaissance — a supremely skilled painter and visionary inventor. He is considered the ideal 'Renaissance man'. Born in 1452 in Vinci, near Florence, he became an apprentice to Andrea del Verrocchio at age 14. He went to work for the Duke of Milan in 1482 as a military engineer. While in Milan, he painted The Virgin of the Rocks and the Last Supper. In 1499 he returned to Florence and painted his most famous work, the Mona Lisa. He dissected corpses to understand the human body and filled thousands of pages of notebooks with sketches. He went to live in France in 1516 and died there.

The Mona Lisa

The Mona Lisa is one of the world's most famous paintings, housed in the Louvre Museum in Paris. It is a portrait of a wealthy Florentine merchant's wife and took over two years to complete. Da Vinci used the sfumato technique to make the skin appear soft, blending colours without hard lines. He also used light and shadow to focus attention on her smile and eyes. The detail on her face shows his detailed knowledge of the human body.

The Last Supper

The Last Supper shows the moment when Jesus tells his disciples that one of them will betray him. It demonstrates da Vinci's excellent use of perspective — the wall behind the table recedes away from the viewer, creating great depth. Da Vinci made Jesus the focal point by having the others positioned around him. Unusually, instead of painting on wet plaster, he experimented with oil and tempera on a dry wall — a mistake, as the paint soon began peeling. Many attempts have since been made to preserve it.

Gentileschi's Judith Slaying Holofernes

This famous painting depicts the biblical story of Judith killing the Assyrian general Holofernes. It displays many elements of Renaissance painting: rich, contrasting colours; close attention to realism in the figures and the gruesome scene; and a black background to focus all attention on the action. It shows a common theme in Gentileschi's work — powerful women doing things considered inappropriate for women at the time.

⚡ Change Maker
Artemisia Gentileschi, 1593–1656

Women in the Renaissance faced huge barriers in pursuing any profession, including the arts. Gentileschi was one of the few women who gained renown for her talent. Her father was a painter and she began working in his workshop. From age 15 she was taking her own commissions. Her work was often presumed to be that of her father or husband. Throughout her career she served as a court painter in Florence (for the Medicis), Rome, Naples and London (for King Charles I). When she was 18, she was assaulted by one of her father's business partners — a traumatic event that may have influenced how she depicted powerful women in her work.

🗿 6.4 Sculpture in the Renaissance
Medieval Sculpture
  • Not very realistic
  • Bodies were long and thin
  • Limbs out of proportion
  • Long, straight clothing reduced detail
  • Attached to churches and monuments
  • Not free-standing
Renaissance Sculpture
  • Known for realism and attention to detail
  • Used real people as models
  • Made detailed drawings and plans beforehand
  • Studied anatomy — some dissected corpses
  • Tended to be naked to show skill and copy ancient Greeks
  • Often free-standing
Michelangelo's David

One of the most famous Renaissance sculptures is David by Michelangelo Buonarroti. In 1501 he won a competition to carve a single huge block of white marble. From it he created the statue of David from the biblical story of David and Goliath, to celebrate Florence defeating France in war. At five metres high, it was the largest free-standing statue since Roman times. David is naked, with a slingshot resting on his shoulder, and his body is very detailed (veins, ligaments, muscles). The statue represents the ideal human body and is evidence that Michelangelo made a close study of anatomy.

⚡ Change Maker
Michelangelo Buonarroti, 1475–1564

Michelangelo was born near Florence in 1475. As a child he showed great interest in sculpture and came to the attention of Lorenzo de Medici, who invited him to study in his sculpture academy. In Rome in 1496, he sculpted the Pietà (meaning 'sorrow') — a sculpture of Mary holding the body of Jesus. In 1501, in Florence, he created the statue of David, his most famous work. In 1508 he began painting the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in the Vatican — a task that took over four years to complete, all while standing or lying on high scaffolding. He also wrote poetry and designed buildings as an architect, including St Peter's Basilica. During his lifetime, Michelangelo was known as 'Il Divino' — the divine one.

🖨️ 6.5 Technological Change: The Printing Press
The Spread of the Renaissance

The Renaissance spread from Italy into the rest of Europe — France, England, Germany and other parts of Europe began to follow the example set by Italy. This was helped by:

  • Visiting Italy and learning directly from Italian masters
  • Trade with Italy, which spread ideas around Europe
  • Northern European rulers and merchants becoming patrons to artists in their own countries
  • The invention of the movable type printing press — considered the single most important cause of the spread of the Renaissance
The Printing Press

Before the 1440s, all books in Europe had to be copied by hand. Because of this, very few books were in circulation — books were very precious and very few people learned to read or write.

📖 Johannes Gutenberg (1398–1468)

Gutenberg was a German goldsmith. Around 1440, he is believed to have invented the movable type printing press. He placed individual metal letters into a frame to form words, coated them with ink and pressed the frame onto paper. The process was repeated as many times as needed. He then moved the letters to make the next page. The first book he printed was the Gutenberg Bible.

Movable Type Printing Press
A method of printing books that placed individual metal letters into a frame to form words, coated them with ink and pressed the frame onto paper. Invented by Gutenberg around 1440.
Impact of the Printing Press

Gutenberg's invention spread quickly — printing presses were operating in every major European city by 1500. The consequences were enormous:

  • Printed books were much cheaper than handwritten manuscripts, allowing more people to afford them
  • More people learned to read and write
  • People read more and were introduced to new ideas
  • Fiction became more popular as people began to read for entertainment
  • The Church's control over learning declined — it was now possible for people to challenge the Church and spread ideas to a wide audience quickly (this would be key to the Reformation)
  • The use of Latin declined as writers wrote in the vernacular — the spoken language of their native country
Vernacular
The language as spoken in a native country. As more people could read, writers began writing in their local spoken language rather than Latin.
⚡ Change Maker
William Shakespeare, 1564–1616

Shakespeare is considered one of the greatest writers in the English language. Born in Stratford-on-Avon in 1564, he moved to London where he joined a company of actors called The King's Men. He wrote his first play, The Comedy of Errors, in 1594. In 1599, the company opened a theatre called The Globe. Shakespeare wrote 38 plays in total. Famous works include: A Midsummer Night's Dream, Much Ado About Nothing, The Merchant of Venice (comedies); Hamlet, Romeo and Juliet, Macbeth (tragedies); Henry V and Julius Caesar (histories).

⚡ Change Maker
Laura Cereta, 1469–1499

Cereta is considered one of the earliest feminist writers and was the most widely read female humanist of the Renaissance. She wrote long letters to notable humanists and political figures arguing against traditional stereotypes about women. She argued that marriage was akin to slavery and advocated for girls' education and for marriage as a partnership of honour, respect, honesty and love. Her letters were collected and published after her death.

🔭 6.6 Health, Medicine and Science in the Renaissance
Medicine during the Renaissance

Medieval medicine was based on some very strange ideas about the body. This began to change in the 1500s when doctors began to investigate anatomy — the study of the structure of the human body.

🫀
Andreas Vesalius (1514–)
Carried out dissections to learn about the body. Wrote On the Fabric of the Human Body — full of correct information and 270 accurate drawings of the human bones, muscles, veins and organs. Transformed medical knowledge.
🩸
William Harvey (1578–1657)
Discovered that the heart pumped blood around the body. Medical treatments such as surgery improved considerably as they were now based on correct information about how the body worked.
Science during the Renaissance

The influence of humanism meant people were very interested in understanding the world around them. The biggest change came in astronomy — the study of the stars. Since ancient times, people believed the Earth was the centre of the solar system and the sun rotated around it. The Catholic Church supported this view, as it fitted the belief that God had created the Earth and humankind as central to the universe.

🔭
Galileo Galilei
A professor of mathematics in Padua, Italy. He heard of the Dutch invention of the telescope and improved it to magnify objects hundreds of times. He discovered that the moon's surface has craters and mountains and that Saturn has rings. He published these in The Starry Messenger in 1610.
🌍
Nicolaus Copernicus
A Polish priest and astronomer who in the 1540s wrote On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, declaring that the Earth rotates around the sun. He did not publish his book until he knew he was dying — he feared the Church's reaction.
The Church and Science

The Catholic Church remained hugely powerful throughout the Renaissance. It opposed any scientific enquiry that went against its teachings. When Galileo backed up Copernicus's ideas in 1632 in Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, the Pope ordered him to be put on trial before the Inquisition (a Church court).

Heresy
Knowingly holding a view that went against the official teachings of the Catholic Church. Galileo was convicted of heresy for supporting the view that the Earth moved around the sun.

Galileo was threatened with being burned at the stake (the usual punishment for heretics) and eventually agreed to say he was wrong. The Church banned his books and he was kept under house arrest until his death.

⚠️
Exam Tip — Galileo Galileo's trial by the Inquisition in the 1630s tells us that the Catholic Church remained very powerful during the Renaissance and was willing to punish those who challenged its teachings, even when the science was correct.
