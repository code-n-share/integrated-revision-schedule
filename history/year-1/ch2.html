⛏️ 2.1 What is Archaeology?

Archaeology is essential to our understanding of history. As we know, history uses many types of source — written, visual, aural and oral. However, only part of history can be told using these sources. We also need evidence for the time before writing — our prehistory — and this comes from material and tactile things such as artefacts and bodies.

Archaeology
The study of the remains left by people in the past. An excavation is when archaeologists dig up the ground to find evidence left by people in the past.
💡 Archaeology is our ONLY source of information about prehistoric times — before writing began.

Archaeologists study evidence carefully so that we can learn from it — like detectives of the past. Archaeology focuses a lot on prehistoric times, but archaeologists also investigate remains from more recent times and work alongside historians to build a complete picture of life in the past.

🗺️ How Archaeologists Find Sites

There are many ways for an object to end up in the ground — dropped, buried, covered by soil over time, or built over. Archaeologists excavate sites for many different reasons:

  • Visible ruins — The ruins of an old building are still visible above the ground and may be worth a closer look. Bective Abbey, Co. Meath is an example.
  • Aerial photography — A photograph taken of the ground from an elevated position (e.g. helicopter or drone). Shapes not obvious at ground level can reveal ancient structures, like the Hill of Tara in Co. Meath.
  • Research archaeology — An old document, map or other record reveals that a building or structure once existed on the site, leading archaeologists to investigate.
  • Rescue archaeology — Before planning permission for a road or building is granted, the site must be checked so that evidence is not lost forever. Before the M3 motorway was built in Co. Meath, 126 sites between Clonee and Kells were excavated.
  • Accidental discovery — Archaeological sites are sometimes found purely by chance. A farmer ploughing a field might find something and report it — that is exactly how the Ardagh Chalice was found.
🛩️
Aerial Photograph
A photograph taken of the ground from an elevated position, such as a helicopter or drone. It reveals shapes and outlines of ancient structures invisible from the ground.
📄
Research Archaeology

🚧
Rescue Archaeology
Before construction of roads or buildings, a site must be checked for evidence so nothing is lost forever. Required by planning law.
🧊 How Evidence is Preserved

Evidence such as bodies and artefacts can be preserved for thousands of years if the conditions are right. As a general rule, when both air and moisture are present, things decay rapidly.

🌡️
Three ways evidence is preserved 1. Extreme heat (deserts) — dries out objects quickly, preventing decay.
2. Airless conditions — airtight coffins, sealed tombs, volcanic ash (e.g. Pompeii) slow decay.
3. European peat bogs — cold, acidic water + airlessness below water level preserves bodies extremely well (e.g. Clonycavan Man).
🏕️ 2.2 The Job of the Archaeologist

When a site worth excavating is found, archaeologists follow a careful sequence of steps to preserve as much evidence as possible.

  1. Carry out a survey — to see if the site is worth excavating.
  2. Dig test trenches — a sample hole dug to see if there is anything of interest present and whether it is worth excavating the whole site. Trenches are measured and marked in grids so that the exact position of anything found can be recorded.
  3. Remove the topsoil using a digger or a pick axe. Topsoil is the uppermost, most recent layer of soil.
  4. Dig very carefully using trowels and shovels to remove smaller amounts of soil without damaging anything.
  5. Use brushes to remove soil delicately from any objects found.
  6. Use sieves to ensure nothing is thrown away in the soil. A sieve catches even the smallest pieces of artefacts.
  7. Record the position of every artefact found. Everything is carefully drawn and photographed.
  8. Catalogue the details of each artefact on computers and in the excavation's site book.
  9. Put artefacts into separate, labelled bags and boxes, which are numbered and sent to the laboratory for tests.
  10. Once tests are finished, artefacts are usually brought to museums, where they can be displayed for people to learn from.
🛠️ Tools Used by Archaeologists
⛏️
Pick Axe & Digger

🔧

🖌️

Used to delicately brush soil away from objects once they have been found, protecting fragile artefacts.

Used to sift through removed soil to ensure no small pieces of artefacts or fragments are accidentally discarded.

🏷️

Artefacts are stored in individually labelled, numbered bags and boxes and sent to the laboratory for further testing.
⚠️
Why must archaeologists be careful? Excavation is irreversible — once the ground is disturbed, you cannot put it back exactly as it was. Any damage done during digging could destroy vital evidence forever. That is why every step is slow, methodical and meticulously recorded.

1. Radiocarbon Dating (Carbon-14 Dating)
Radiocarbon Dating
A method of dating based on the falling levels of carbon-14 in tissue over time. All living things contain carbon-14. After death, the level drops at a steady rate — so the less carbon-14 remains, the older the tissue.
💡 The older the tissue, the less carbon-14 it contains.
📍 Irish Example — Mount Sandel, Co. Derry (Mesolithic: 8,000–3,500 BC)

In the 1970s, archaeologists found Mesolithic evidence at Mount Sandel in Co. Derry. Mesolithic means 'of the Middle Stone Age'. The first people came to Ireland during the Mesolithic period, probably from Scotland across to Northern Ireland on wooden boats.

Most of Ireland was covered in dense forest, so the earliest settlements were near the coast or rivers. At this time, people were hunter-gatherers — they hunted animals for food and gathered berries and nuts, but had not yet learned how to farm.

The archaeologists excavating the site found animal and bird bones and the shells of hazelnuts. Using radiocarbon dating, these shells were tested to establish a date for the site. They date from 7,000 BC — so Mount Sandel is between 9,000 and 10,000 years old.

🔄 Reinterpreting the Past

A recent discovery in a Co. Clare cave of a brown bear's bone with human cut marks on it indicates that people were already living in Ireland 2,500 years before Mount Sandel suggests! This is a good example of reinterpretation and is being studied closely now.

2. Geophysical Surveying
Geophysical Survey
Like an X-ray of the ground. Maps and images of any archaeological evidence underground are made without an excavation. It can locate artefacts, buried buildings and structures.
📍 Irish Example — Newgrange Passage Tomb, Co. Meath (Neolithic: 3,500–2,000 BC)

A passage tomb is a narrow passage with one or more burial chambers, made of large stones and covered in earth or stone. Other Neolithic passage tombs at Brú na Bóinne include Knowth and Dowth. Neolithic means 'of the New Stone Age' — when the first farmers came to Ireland.

Until 1962, Newgrange had not been excavated properly. Inside is a passage 20 metres long, which leads into a 6-metre high central chamber with three sections. The aim of the geophysical survey was to see whether there were any hidden passageways or chambers, but none were discovered.

The central chamber has a corbelled roof — a domed roof built by overlapping stones until they meet at the top. A capstone was placed over this. To this day, no water leaks into the chamber.

Outside, a series of stones surrounds the passage tomb in a circle, decorated with diamond and spiral carvings. Above the entrance is a gap known as a roof box. Every 21 December — the winter solstice — the rising sun shines through the roof box and right along the passage, all the way into the chamber.

💡 Did You Know?

Newgrange is older than the Pyramids in Egypt. Newgrange is estimated to date from between 3,000 and 2,500 BC. The Pyramids are estimated to date from between 2,600 and 2,500 BC.

3. Pollen Analysis
Pollen Analysis
The study of pollen remains to find out what was growing at a site during a particular time period. Archaeologists have records of when certain pollens were common, so they can match pollen found during excavation to the correct period.
💡 Pollen analysis can date objects AND tell us when forests were cleared and farming began in a particular area.
📍 Irish Example — The Céide Fields, Co. Mayo (Neolithic: 3,500–2,000 BC)

The Céide Fields were discovered by accident in the 1930s by a teacher who, while cutting turf in the bog, noticed a pile of stones. Buried underneath the bog, archaeologists found stone walls stretching for several kilometres. People lived here between 4,000 and 3,000 BC.

These Neolithic people were mainly dairy farmers. The walls show they were organised, protected their animals from wild animals and divided up the land amongst themselves.

The pollen analysis showed a dramatic drop in tree pollens, proving that pine forest areas were cleared to create fields for farming. Among the interesting objects found were a primitive plough, a quern used for grinding corn, some arrowheads and pieces of pottery.

The blanket bog that grew over the fields preserved the site, leaving it in excellent condition for archaeologists to study.

4. Stratigraphy
Stratigraphy
A method of dating artefacts and evidence by how deep in the ground they were when found. The deeper they are, the older they will be.
💡 Think of it like layers of a cake — the bottom layer was put down first, so it's the oldest. This method is used in most archaeological excavations.

If you were to drop something today, it would lie on the surface, but over thousands of years it would become covered by soil, leaves and other matter and eventually end up buried many metres deep.

5. Dendrochronology (Tree-Ring Dating)
Dendrochronology
Also called tree-ring dating. A method of dating that uses the unique growth patterns of tree rings as a guide. Each ring represents one year of growth; rings are wider when the tree grew quickly (e.g. a good summer).

Archaeologists have created a continuous record of tree-ring patterns dating back to 5,300 BC. This makes it possible to find out when a tree was cut down to make an artefact or structure, and so it can be used to date wooden objects. However, well-preserved wood is needed for this, which is not found on most sites — so this is not a widely used method.

6. DNA Testing, 3D Reconstruction & Bone Analysis

Archaeologists sometimes find bodies when they excavate sites. Modern methods reveal a lot about these bodies:

🧬
DNA Testing
DNA can tell us about the origins and ethnicity of a person — in other words, where they came from and who their ancestors were.
🖥️
3D Reconstruction
Using computer modelling and then clay to reconstruct part of a body. If most of a skull is found, for example, a person's face can be reconstructed based on their skull structure.
3D reconstructions have been completed of skulls from Pompeii and Herculaneum, preserved by the AD 79 eruption of Mount Vesuvius.
🦴
Bone Analysis
Bones reveal a huge amount of information: gender (pelvis), age (teeth), height (thigh bone), cause of death (damage), disease or poor nutrition, and diet (skin/hair analysis).
🔄 Reinterpreting the Past — Cheddar Man

In February 2018, researchers analysing the DNA of 'Cheddar Man' — who lived in south-west England 10,000 years ago — discovered he had bright blue eyes, dark brown to black skin and dark curly hair. Ireland's Mesolithic people would have looked just like Cheddar Man. Pale skin is a genetic adaptation that happened slowly over the millennia to help people get more vitamin D from the sun in cloudy, rainy northern regions.

📍 Irish Example — Clonycavan Man (Iron Age: 500 BC–AD 400)

Clonycavan Man's body is believed to be 2,300 years old, dating from the Iron Age — the period when people made tools and weapons from iron. He was found in a Co. Meath peat bog in 2003. Only his head and upper body were preserved.

He had crooked teeth and a thin beard. His diet consisted mostly of fruit and vegetables. His hair style was very distinctive — he used an ancient hair gel made of plant oil and pine resin. Its ingredients came from France and Spain, which shows he was wealthy. Archaeologists think he may have been a king and was ritually sacrificed.

His skull was split by a sharp object, and there was a large cut across his nose and under his right eye. He was estimated to be aged 24–40 when he died. The methods used to study him included bone analysis, DNA testing and 3D facial reconstruction.

🛡️ Conservation
Conservation
When historic objects are protected and preserved so that they do not decay. Objects found by archaeologists can be displayed under the correct conditions in museums and heritage sites.
  • Objects found by archaeologists can be displayed under the correct conditions in museums and heritage sites.
  • Documents, maps and photographs can be safely stored in archives and libraries.
  • Historic buildings are looked after and restored when necessary so they stay in good condition.
🌟
Why is conservation important? Conserving the past benefits us all — we can continue to learn about and engage with our history. It preserves artefacts for future generations and ensures our shared heritage is not lost forever.
