⚔️ 5.1 The Normans

The Middle Ages was the period of European history from about 500 to around 1500 — from the end of the Roman Empire to the start of the Renaissance. It was a time when war and violence were widespread, and life was especially difficult for people at the lower levels of society.

The Normans
After the fall of Rome, there was no single strong ruler in Europe. The stronger rulers gathered more territory and became kings — they organised society under the feudal system. The Normans were among the most powerful of these groups. While the Vikings were settling in Ireland, they were also settling in north-west France. This area became known as Normandy.

In 1066, the King of England, Edward the Confessor, died without an heir. William, the Duke of Normandy, claimed the English Crown — but this was rejected by the English nobles, led by Harold, Earl of Hereford. William invaded England and defeated Harold at the Battle of Hastings. William became King of England and was known as William the Conqueror. The story of the Norman Conquest is told in the Bayeux Tapestry.

By 1200, different groups of Normans had set out to conquer many different parts of Europe. They eventually controlled land in England, Ireland, France, Italy, Tunisia, Libya and the Middle East.

Feudalism
Feudalism
The system of land ownership where rulers divided land among their followers in return for loyalty and taxes. Followers were called nobles or vassals and were given land called a fief. They swore an oath of fealty to the king, promising to fight for him, provide troops, and pay taxes.

🏰 5.2 Life in a Norman Castle

The Normans needed a way to control their newly conquered territory and people. William the Conqueror divided the land among his followers. In order to take control of a new fief quickly, a lord would first build a temporary castle called a motte and bailey.

⛰️
Motte and Bailey Castle
A motte was a small artificial hill with a wooden tower or keep at the top. Below was a large enclosed area — the bailey — surrounded by high wooden fences and a ditch or moat.
Temporary structure — once control was established, a permanent stone castle was built.
🧱
Stone Castles
Medieval stone castles were huge fortifications, taking several hundred men working for several years to complete. They had curtain walls, a keep, dungeons, battlements, towers/turrets, the bailey, and a gatehouse.
Key defensive features: high walls, narrow windows, battlements for soldiers to patrol.
🚪
Gatehouse
Found in the gatehouse were the drawbridge (raised and lowered to control entry; raised at night or under attack) and the portcullis (a metal grid lowered over the gate if necessary).
💧
Moat
The castle was surrounded by a deep ditch filled with water, called the moat. This made it extremely difficult for attackers to approach the castle walls.
Castles and War

Warfare was widespread during the Middle Ages. Castles were key to the defence of a lord's land and a kingdom generally. Even if an attacking army surrounded the castle (a siege), the castle could hold out for a long time if it had enough food stores and a well. Defenders could fire arrows from the walls, or pour boiling water or oil down on attackers.

Laying siege was difficult and time-consuming. Attacking armies used catapults and siege towers. If these failed, attackers would tunnel under the castle walls to undermine them. When gunpowder and the cannon were brought from China to Europe in the 1400s, castle walls could be easily destroyed — and castles were no longer key to warfare.

Lords and Ladies

A Norman lord was very powerful. He owned huge areas of land and commanded many knights and soldiers. A Norman lady usually married at a young age. Marriages were arranged by the two families, often to create an alliance. A dowry (usually money or land) was paid to the groom.

The lady's main duty was to bear children as heirs. The family lived upstairs in the keep, near the chapel and a warm sunny room called a solar. The lady would weave, teach the children, play music, and also run the household — instructing servants, supervising storage and preparation of food, and acting for her husband when he was away.

Feasts were held in the great hall, which had big fireplaces and tapestries. Beef, pork, mutton, duck, deer, pheasant or rabbit were served. Without refrigeration, people salted, smoked, or dried meat. Instead of plates, people often ate from large pieces of hard bread called trenchers. Minstrels (musicians) and jesters (comic performers) provided entertainment. Clothing embroidered or dyed in vivid colours showed off wealth.

🗡️ 5.3 Soldiers in the Middle Ages

The Norman army that conquered England was made up of three main types of soldiers:

🪖
Foot Soldiers
Made up the largest part of any medieval army. They fought with swords, daggers and spears, and had shields and leather padded jackets for protection. They were peasants who returned to the fields when the battle or war was over.
🏹
Archers
Fought with a bow and arrows, or sometimes a longbow or crossbow. They wore little protective clothing. Norman archers in England were considered the best in Europe and gave their side a major advantage in battles.
🐴
Knights
Minor nobles who fought on horseback and swore an oath of chivalry. They wore full body armour (plate armour), chainmail, a shield and a helmet, and used lances, swords and maces. Only a few hundred knights existed, but they proved decisive at the Battle of Hastings in 1066.
The Training of a Knight

There were two ways for a man to become a knight. If a foot soldier showed exceptional bravery on the battlefield, he could be knighted by a lord — though this was very rare. The main way was a three-stage process followed by the sons of nobles:

  1. Stage 1: Page — At the age of seven, a boy was sent to live with the family of another lord. He would learn to ride a horse, use a sword, sing and dance. He was taught manners by the lady of the castle and served the lord and lady at table.
  2. Stage 2: Squire — At around 14, the boy began learning to fight on horseback. He would accompany the lord into battle, look after his horse and weapons, and help the lord dress for battle and tournaments.
  3. Stage 3: Knight — At the age of 21, he was ready to become a knight. He spent the night before the ceremony in prayer in the chapel. Then, dressed in white and full armour, he underwent the ceremony of dubbing. He swore the oath of chivalry (to stay loyal to, protect the poor and weak, and be brave in battle). The lord touched him on both shoulders with a sword, saying "Arise, sir knight." The lord would give a manor to the knight to manage. When not fighting in wars and battles, a knight would often take part in tournaments — events featuring mock battles and jousting contests.
Oath of Chivalry
Sworn by a knight: to stay loyal to his lord, protect the poor and weak, and be brave in battle.
Exam tip: Know all three parts of the oath — loyalty, protection, bravery.

🌾 5.4 Life in the Norman Countryside

The vast majority of people in the Middle Ages lived in the countryside. Most were peasants and lived in villages called manors that were owned by a lord or knight. Each manor was quite small, made up of about 50 houses for peasants, as well as the manor house of the lord or knight and the church of the local priest.

A manor usually had a mill for grinding wheat to make bread, a blacksmith's forge, and a house for the bailiff — the man who ran the manor in the lord's absence and was responsible for collecting taxes and keeping law and order in the village. The peasants were strictly forbidden to hunt in the nearby woodland — if caught, they could lose a hand!

Farming in the Middle Ages — The Open Field System
  • The land for crops was divided into three huge fields. Each field was divided into long strips tended by different families. Each family had strips in each of the three fields.
  • They practised crop rotation — the crop planted in each field changed each year.
  • A fourth large field called the commons was used for grazing the animals that belonged to the peasants.

Peasants in the Middle Ages: Freemen and Serfs
🧑‍🌾
Freemen
Had to pay rent to the lord and pay a tithe (a tenth of their income) to the local priest — but did not have to work the land for free. They could marry and leave the manor as they wished.
⛓️
Serfs
Essentially belonged to their lord. They worked six days a week farming the lord's lands. They could not leave the manor or marry without the lord's permission. They paid taxes to the lord and a tithe to the priest.
If a serf escaped and stayed free for a year and a day, he became a freeman.
How Serfs Lived

A serf's home was tiny — a single room with wattle and daub walls, an earthen floor and a thatched roof. Inside was cramped, dark and smoky. At night, animals were kept inside for warmth. Men wore tunics and leggings; women wore long dresses with a head scarf. Their main diet was bread, cheese and a vegetable and oat soup called pottage. They drank ale (a weak beer made from barley) because it was safer to drink than water. They ate meat very rarely, possibly only at Christmas and Easter. Leisure time was only on Sundays and saints' days — there was singing, dancing, drinking and village games.

📖 Did You Know?

The average life expectancy for a child born in England in 1300 was just 30–33 years. In 2000, it was 77–79 years.

🏘️ 5.5 Life in a Town in Norman England

Towns in Norman England and around Europe in the Middle Ages were much smaller than towns today — most had about 1,000 people. Many were built on rivers or coasts so people could make money from trade, and sometimes near castles to cater to castle needs. A town needed a charter from the king — this was a contract granting the town freedom to run its own affairs but requiring it to pay taxes to the king. A mayor ran the town.

Key Features of a Medieval Town
  • High walls to protect against attack.
  • Strong gates — the only way in and out. Anyone wanting to conduct business had to pay a toll (a tax on entry).
  • A main/high street ran from the main gates to the market square in the middle — the only wide, paved street. The richest people lived here.
  • Narrow streets ran off the main street with houses and shops for townspeople.
  • Larger towns had several churches and even a cathedral if a bishop was based there.
  • Outside the walls was the fair green for the annual fair — a huge market where traders from all over the country sold silks, spices, tools and weapons.
Houses and Streets

The average person's house was made of wood, usually several storeys high. The higher floors stuck out over the street, blocking the sun and making streets very dark. Only important buildings — the church, town hall and richer homes — were made of stone. Craftsmen had their shops on the ground floor, with their family living above. Houses had no toilets; people used chamber pots. Fire was a constant danger due to all the wooden buildings — towns imposed curfews requiring people to extinguish their fires at sundown. Streets were little more than mud tracks with an open drain down the middle, making the spread of disease very easy.

Craftsmen and Guilds

Craftsmen produced everyday goods — examples include butchers, bakers, blacksmiths, carpenters, stonemasons, coopers (barrel-makers), weavers and glovers (leather-makers). As most could not read, their shop sign was a picture, not words.

Guild
An organisation of people who worked in the same trade. Craftsmen of each town formed guilds — charged a fee for membership — which set standards for quality, set prices and wages, decided who could trade in the town, and looked after sick members and families of dead members.
Becoming a Master Craftsman — Three Stages
  1. Apprentice (age ~12) — lived with a master craftsman, slept in the workshop, received no pay but was often treated harshly. After about seven years, he became a journeyman.
  2. Journeyman — must be paid for his work and could travel to different workshops and towns for work and experience.
  3. Master Craftsman — eventually applied to the guild. He created a masterpiece (the finest example of his work). If the guild accepted it, he could open his own workshop, train apprentices and sell his work.

⛪ 5.6 Religion in the Middle Ages
Christendom
Medieval Europe was almost entirely Catholic. Religious belief was very strong and had a huge influence on people's daily lives. Many people lived in constant fear of going to hell. So powerful was the Catholic Church that Europe was often called Christendom, meaning the "kingdom of Christ."

The Pope in Rome was the head of the Catholic Church and the most powerful ruler in Europe. Europe was divided into dioceses by archbishops and bishops, and then into smaller parishes, run by priests. Kings often gave land and money to bishops, making the Church very wealthy. The most important churchman in Norman England was the Archbishop of Canterbury.

Priests in the Middle Ages

Every village and town made up a parish. The local priest was rare in being able to read and write, so he was often asked to write letters or advise people on legal problems. Priests gave children a very basic education focused on the Bible. They said Mass every Sunday, tended to the sick, and oversaw marriages and baptisms. As each peasant had to pay the priest a tithe every year, most priests were quite wealthy. The Church helped maintain order by encouraging people to accept their hard lives and look forward to getting their reward in heaven.

Monasteries

Some people wanted to withdraw from the world to focus on prayer and be closer to God. They set up monasteries (for men, who became monks) and convents (for women, who became nuns). These were closed communities. An abbot was head of a monastery; an abbess of a convent.

Monasteries were very important in medieval life — they were the first places to offer education based on ability, regardless of wealth. They provided help to the poor, the sick, and shelter to travellers. They also copied books in the scriptorium.

🛏️
Dormitory
A large room where the monks slept.
🍽️
Refectory
The room where the monks ate.
🚶
Cloisters
Covered walkways used for prayer and walking.
📖
Chapter House
Where the monks gathered for readings.
🏥
Infirmary
Where the infirmarian looked after the ill of the monastery and nearby community.
🏠
Hostel
Guests and travellers could stay here, managed by the hosteller.
Becoming a Monk

A boy who wanted to become a monk would join a monastery as a novice around the age of 15 (though much younger children could be sent). He was taught to live by the Rule of St Benedict. He learned to read and write in Latin and would study Church teachings and history.

After a number of years, if accepted, he swore three solemn vows: poverty (could not own anything), chastity (could not get married), and obedience (must do what the abbot told him). The crown of his head was shaved — called a tonsure — and he received his habit (monks' garments). Two major monastic orders were the Benedictines and the Cistercians.

Some monks preferred to live among the people. These monks, called friars, travelled around the countryside or set up friaries in towns. Famous orders include the Franciscans, the Augustinians and the Dominicans.

☠️ 5.7 Health and Medicine in the Middle Ages

Medieval medicine was based on the writings of the Ancient Greeks. Their beliefs about the body were very far from modern medicine.

Four Humours (Hippocrates)
Hippocrates, the "Father of Medicine", believed the body was made up of four humours: blood, black bile, yellow bile and phlegm. If a person was sick, it was due to an imbalance between these humours.
🩸
Bleeding
Cutting the patient and draining some blood to restore the balance of humours.
🫙
Cupping
Placing heated metal cups on the skin to draw fluids to the surface.
🐛
Leeching
Using leeches to draw blood or other fluids out of the body.
🪚
Amputation
Cutting off a limb to stop infection from spreading throughout the body.

Herbal medicine was also common, with mixtures of herbs given to the sick. Monasteries specialised in the care of the sick and functioned as the first hospitals. Medieval conditions were cramped and filthy, and people lacked the basics of good sanitation. Even minor wounds often became infected and could kill. Diseases widespread in the Middle Ages included typhoid, leprosy, smallpox, puerperal fever and dysentery.

The Black Death — ⚠️ MUST KNOW
⚠️
This is a key exam topic — know it well! The Black Death is one of the most important topics in this chapter. Be able to describe: what it was, how it spread, symptoms, how people responded, and its impact.
The Black Death (Bubonic Plague) — 1347–1359
The worst disease to affect Europe in the Middle Ages was the bubonic plague. It is estimated to have killed at least one-third of Europe's population and became known as the Black Death. The plague was carried by fleas, thought to have first arrived via ships' rats from the Black Sea area, spreading along the trade network throughout Europe.
AspectDetail
Cause Bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas on rats. Isolated in 1894; confirmed as the Black Death through burial analysis in 2010.
Symptoms Oozing swellings (buboes) all over the body, darkly discoloured skin, filling of the lungs with phlegm. Once infected, a 75% chance of dying within a week.
How it spread Extremely contagious — contracted by sneezing, spitting, or touching dead bodies. Spread quickly in crowded, dirty towns.
How people responded Believed it was a result of God's anger at human sin — sought to cure it through prayer, fasting or beating themselves with whips. Others blamed outsiders, especially Jewish communities.
Impact The feudal system went into decline; serfs left manors to replace those who died. Peasants who remained could demand better treatment, reduced taxes and more land. Doctors began to question their practices, leading to big changes in medicine during the Renaissance.

🍀 5.8 The Normans in Ireland

In 1167 the King of Leinster, Dermot MacMurrough, was stripped of his kingdom by Rory O'Connor, the High King of Ireland. MacMurrough asked King Henry II of England for help in regaining his lands, offering to become Henry's vassal in return. Henry allowed him to recruit soldiers from among his Norman lords.

MacMurrough made a deal with Richard de Clare (nicknamed Strongbow) for a Norman army: Strongbow would marry MacMurrough's daughter Aoife and become King of Leinster upon MacMurrough's death.

In 1169, MacMurrough invaded Ireland with a force of 40 knights, 500 foot soldiers and 360 archers. They easily defeated the Vikings of Wexford. The Normans were successful in their military campaigns because of their use of horses and archers and their better armour and swords. In 1170, Strongbow arrived with 200 knights and 1,000 foot soldiers. He and MacMurrough marched on Dublin, taking the city and ending Viking power in Ireland.

In 1171 MacMurrough died and Strongbow became King of Leinster. Henry II landed in September 1171 with 500 knights and 4,000 soldiers to establish himself as ruler of Ireland. Many Irish kings swore allegiance to Henry, who gave himself the title of 'Lord of Ireland'.

The Impact of Norman Rule on Ireland
  • 🏴󠁧󠁢󠁥󠁮󠁧󠁿 The Normans introduced English rule to Ireland — it would last for nearly 800 years.
  • 🏰 The feudal system was introduced to Ireland.
  • 🗼 The Normans built big castles (e.g. Trim, Carrickfergus, Kilkenny). Towns, manors and villages grew up around them.
  • 🌾 Farming practices shifted from herding cattle to growing crops.
  • ⚖️ English Common Law replaced the old Gaelic Brehon Law system.
  • 📛 Norman names became very common in Ireland — names with 'Fitz' (meaning 'son of'), and surnames Burke, Butler, Browne and Barry are Norman names.

However, the Gaelic Irish also influenced the Normans. Many Norman families intermarried with native Irish (Gaelic) chieftains. The new families became known as the Anglo-Irish or 'Old English'. They adopted the Irish language, dressed like the Gaelic Irish and followed Irish customs and laws — becoming, as one English official noted, "more Irish than the Irish themselves." Several failed attempts were made to ban these practices, such as the Statutes of Kilkenny in 1367.

The Normans never fully conquered Ireland. Gaelic rule remained strong in the north and west. Over time, the Anglo-Irish lords became increasingly independent. Direct English control was limited to an area surrounding Dublin known as the Pale.

🌍 5.9 The Legacy of the Normans
👑
The British Royal Family
When William the Conqueror became King of England in 1066, he established a new ruling dynasty. All kings and queens of England since then are directly descended from him.
🏛️
Architecture
The Normans made significant contributions to architecture across England and Ireland. They introduced Romanesque and Gothic styles into church building, meaning much bigger buildings with higher roofs could be built. Christ Church Cathedral in Dublin is an example.
🇫🇷
Language
After 1066, French became the language of government in England, as Normans replaced the Anglo-Saxon nobility. French heavily influenced the development of the English language — over 10,000 words in modern-day English have their roots in French.
✝️
The Crusades
Norman knights played important parts in many wars and battles across Europe and the Mediterranean. Beginning in 1095 and concluding in 1291, the Crusades were a series of invasions of the Middle East by European Christians to wrest control of the Holy Land from its Muslim rulers.

Feudalism Land ownership system: King → Lords → Knights → Peasants. Key word: fief (land granted). Draw the pyramid! Know the oath of fealty.
Motte & Bailey Temporary castle: motte = artificial hill with keep; bailey = enclosed area below. Distinguish motte and bailey clearly.
Stone Castle Key parts: curtain walls, keep, dungeons, battlements, bailey, gatehouse, moat, portcullis, drawbridge. Know at least 5 castle features and their purpose.
Knights 3 stages: Page (7) → Squire (14) → Knight (21). Swore oath of chivalry. Fought on horseback. Know all 3 stages AND what happens at each.
Open Field System Three fields, crop rotation, fallow year, commons field for grazing. Know what "fallow" means and why it was used.
Serfs vs Freemen Serfs: belonged to lord, 6 days' work, couldn't leave without permission. Freemen: paid rent but free to leave. Two key differences: ownership of freedom + work obligations.
Medieval Towns Charter, high walls, toll gates, market square, guilds. Houses wooden, fire a constant danger. Explain why disease spread easily — filthy streets, cramped houses.
Christendom Catholic Europe; Pope most powerful; Church divided into dioceses > parishes. Flying buttresses = Gothic innovation. Know difference between Romanesque and Gothic cathedrals.
Black Death 1347–1359. Bubonic plague. Killed 1/3 of Europe. Fleas on rats. Symptoms: buboes, discoloured skin. Know cause, spread, symptoms, response, and impact on feudalism.
Normans in Ireland 1169 invasion. MacMurrough + Strongbow. Henry II 1171. Impact: English rule, feudalism, castles, Common Law, Norman names. Know three impacts of Norman rule on Ireland.
Legacy British royal family descended from William. 10,000+ French words in English. The Crusades. Christ Church Cathedral, Dublin = Norman architecture in Ireland.
