🗺️ The 7 Steps to Writing a Short Story

Follow these steps in order to plan and write a strong, well-structured short story.

1
Brainstorm
Let your imagination run wild. Write down ideas, create mind maps, list possible events or characters. Don't filter yourself at this stage — everything is worth considering.
Tip: Think about a character, a problem, and a setting first.
2
The Plot
The plot is what your story revolves around. It has four parts: (1) Beginning/Exposition, (2) Middle/Conflict + Rising Action, (3) Climax, (4) End/Resolution.
Always start in the middle of the action — don't waste your opening lines on background!
3
The Setting
The setting is where and when the action takes place — e.g. the past, present or future; countryside, city, a mansion, a slum. Setting affects character and gives hints about what has happened or what's going to happen.
Setting can reveal a character's background and foreshadow events.
4
The Characters
Keep character numbers small — 3 or 4 max for a short story. You need a protagonist (leading character), an antagonist (who opposes the protagonist), and possibly a supporting character (sidekick, best friend, love interest) who develops the main characters.
Give the reader someone to root for — make them care about your protagonist!
5
The Theme
The theme is the central message of your story — the main point the writer wants the reader to take away. It's not the plot (what happens), it's the meaning (what it's really about). e.g. friendship, courage, loss, identity.
6
The Conflict
Conflict is the problem the main character must solve. The plot revolves around this conflict. It can be internal (the character battling themselves — emotions, fears, decisions) or external (battling another person, nature, society, or circumstances).
7
Be Descriptive — Use Your Five Senses
Transport your reader into the story. Use sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch. Use your poetic techniques (simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, assonance, imagery) to bring your writing to life.
Good descriptive writing doesn't just say "it was dark" — it makes the reader feel the darkness.
📈 Plot Structure Diagram

The Short Story Plot Diagram

⭐
Always Start in the Middle! Don't start at the beginning of your story — start right in the action. Your opening line should grab the reader immediately. The backstory (what happened before) can be revealed gradually as the story unfolds.
①
Rising Action + Conflict
Your story begins here — in the middle of action. Introduce characters and the central problem. Build tension.
②
Climax
The peak of the story — the most dramatic, tense, or emotional moment. Everything has been building to this.
③
Back Story
Reveal the background — who the characters are, how they got here. This is revealed after the climax or woven in naturally.
④
Falling Action
The tension drops after the climax. The main conflict begins to be resolved. Consequences become clear.
⑤
Resolution
The story ends. Loose ends are tied up — though not always with a happy ending. Leave the reader satisfied (or thoughtfully unsettled).
🏙️ Setting & Characters
Types of Characters
Protagonist
The leading character in the story — the one the reader follows. We usually root for them. Their decisions drive the plot forward.
Antagonist
The character who contends with or opposes the protagonist. They create the conflict. Not always a villain — could be a force or circumstance.
Supporting Character
Adds to the character development of the protagonist or antagonist. e.g. the sidekick, best friend, love interest, mentor, comic relief.
⚡ Conflict — Internal vs External

Every good story has conflict. The conflict is the central problem that drives the plot. It can be internal, external, or both.

🧠 Internal Conflict
The character is battling themselves — their own emotions, fears, doubts, or decisions. This creates a psychological tension the reader can relate to.

Example: A character torn between doing what is right and what is easy.
⚔️ External Conflict
The character is battling something outside themselves — another person, nature, society, fate, or circumstances beyond their control.

Example: A character being chased, facing a storm, or fighting injustice.
🎨 Descriptive Writing — Five Senses

The key to powerful creative writing is to transport the reader into your story. Use all five senses to make your descriptions vivid and immersive.

👁️
Sight (Visual)
"The amber sky blazed like a furnace above the rooftops."
👂
Sound (Aural)
"The creak of the floorboard cut through the silence."
✋
Touch (Tactile)
"The rough bark scraped against her palms as she climbed."
👃
Smell (Olfactory)
"The metallic tang of gunpowder mingled with the salt of her sweat."
👅
Taste (Gustatory)
"Fear tasted like copper at the back of his throat."
Use Your Poetic Techniques
Techniques to use in creative writing:
  • Simile — "A bomb blew bits of him everywhere, a fully grown man now reduced to small pieces falling from the sky like confetti."
  • Alliteration — "bits… blew… bomb" — makes writing punchy and musical.
  • Metaphor — "She was a hurricane of emotion, destroying everything in her path."
  • Personification — "The fog crept through the streets like a thief."
  • Repetition — "He ran. He ran until his lungs screamed. He ran until the world disappeared."
  • Imagery — "I could taste the metallic tang of gunpowder, mingled with the salt of my sweat, my breath heaving from my chest as I ran."
💬 Dialogue

Dialogue brings your story to life. Use it to reveal character, advance the plot, and create tension. It should sound natural — like people actually speak.

What Good Dialogue Does:
Make it sound natural
Show characters' personality
Convey emotions
Include descriptions — tone, body language, gestures
Advance the plot
Create tension or humour
Example 1:
"Oh no. We're going to die," I said, rolling my eyes.
"Could you be more sarcastic!" yelled Bob.

Example 2:
"His suit trousers ripped the whole way up the seam," I said sadly.
"Oh wow!" said my friend, holding back her laughter. "What happened next?" she said, almost at the brink of bursting.

Example 3:
"Due to unfortunate events, she is no longer coming," said my dad.
"What do you mean?" I said, angrily and shocked. "She's the bride!"
🏆 Top Tips for Short Stories
Short Story Checklist
  1. Start in the middle of the action — don't waste the opening with background. If you're writing about aliens landing on Earth, start as they're about to land.
  2. Keep up the pace — don't let the story drag. Every sentence should do something: reveal character, create tension, or advance the plot.
  3. Keep the number of characters small — 3 or 4 maximum for a short story.
  4. Give the reader someone to root for — make the protagonist likeable or interesting enough to care about.
  5. Create conflict — a story without a problem isn't a story. The conflict is the engine.
  6. Appeal to the five senses — transport the reader into your story with vivid sensory details.
  7. Use dialogue — it will bring your story to life and make your characters feel real.
  8. Check grammar and punctuation — especially dialogue punctuation and tenses.
✍️ Strong Opening Example
"I could taste the metallic tang of gunpowder, mingled with the salt of my sweat, my breath heaving from my chest as I ran. A bomb blew bits of him everywhere — a fully grown man, now reduced to small pieces falling from the sky like confetti. I didn't stop running."

🧠 Quick Quiz — Test Yourself
What are the four stages of a plot?
Plot
1. Beginning/Exposition — introduce characters and situation. 2. Middle/Conflict + Rising Action — the problem develops, tension builds. 3. Climax — the peak moment, everything comes to a head. 4. End/Resolution — the conflict is resolved. Remember: always start your story in the middle of the action!
What is the difference between internal and external conflict?
Conflict
Internal conflict = the character is battling themselves — their own emotions, fears, or decisions. e.g. choosing between loyalty and honesty. External conflict = the character is battling something outside themselves — another person, nature, society. e.g. being chased or fighting injustice. Good stories often have BOTH.
Name the five types of imagery and give a creative example for each.
Descriptive Writing
1. Visual (sight) — "The amber sky blazed above the rooftops." 2. Aural (sound) — "The creak of the floorboard split the silence." 3. Tactile (touch) — "The rough bark scraped her palms." 4. Olfactory (smell) — "The metallic tang of gunpowder hung in the air." 5. Gustatory (taste) — "Fear tasted like copper at the back of his throat."
What are the three types of characters? Define each.
Characters
Protagonist — the leading character; the reader follows their journey and usually roots for them. Antagonist — the character who contends with or opposes the protagonist; they create conflict. Supporting character — adds to the character development of the protagonist or antagonist (sidekick, best friend, love interest, mentor).
What is the theme of a story, and how is it different from the plot?
Theme
The plot is WHAT happens — the sequence of events. The theme is WHAT IT MEANS — the central message the author wants the reader to take away. e.g. Plot: a boy runs away from an orphanage. Theme: hope / the will to survive. A strong theme gives your story depth and meaning beyond just the events.
Name 4 things good dialogue should do in a story.
Dialogue
Dialogue should: 1. Sound natural — like real people talking. 2. Show character personality — how someone speaks reveals who they are. 3. Convey emotions — through word choice and tone. 4. Include descriptions — body language, gesture, and tone (she said hesitantly / he snapped). 5. Advance the plot or create tension/humour.
