🏭 What Are Secondary Economic Activities?
Primary economic activities are the extraction of raw materials. Secondary economic activities take raw materials and manufacture a product.
💡 Primary = digging the potatoes out of the ground. Secondary = turning them into crisps in a factory.

Every factory can be described as a flowchart of Inputs → Processes → Outputs:

  1. Raw materials / ingredients are the inputs in any factory.
  2. They are processed to become products.
  3. Finished products are the outputs.
🧩 Remember it: I-P-O: In (inputs), Process, Out (outputs).
⚠️ Common mistake: forgetting that waste is also an output, and that labour (workers) is an input.
📍 Factors Influencing the Location of Industry
A factory needs to choose its location carefully. Different factors affect location, and depending on the product, some factors may be more important than others (e.g. if raw materials are bulky and hard to transport).
💡 It’s like choosing where to open a shop: you weigh up cost, customers, helpers and rules, and one of them usually matters most.
FactorExplanation
Raw materialsIf they are bulky or heavy, the factory moves closer to them to cut transportation costs.
TransportTo receive materials and ship out products you need access to transport links by air, sea, road and rail.
ServicesWater, electricity, WiFi and broadband need to be easily accessible.
LabourBeing close to towns/cities provides a workforce; universities provide a skilled workforce. Skilled = requires third-level qualifications. Semi-skilled = trained on the job.
MarketWhere the finished product is sold; a factory might locate near its target market.
LinkagesCompanies that rely on products made by another company may locate near each other, e.g. a bakery near a flour mill.
CapitalMoney to cover the cost of building, paying workers and transport. From investors, banks or the government.
Government policyThe Irish government has agencies to help companies set up in Ireland or abroad: they give loans and grants; the IDA (Industrial Development Authority) works to attract industry; Ireland has a 12.5% corporation tax.
Personal preferencesThe person setting up the business may simply prefer to locate somewhere, e.g. starting a business in their home town.
🧩 Remember it: R-T-S-L-M-L-C-G-P = Raw materials, Transport, Services, Labour, Market, Linkages, Capital, Government, Personal. Silly sentence: “Really Tall Sheep Like Mixing Lovely Cold Green Peas.”
⚠️ Common mistake: naming a factor without explaining it. Say why it matters: “Raw materials: bulky and heavy, so locating near them cuts transport costs.”
⚖️ Types of Industry
TypeProducts / materialsExample
Heavy industryRaw materials and products that are bulky and heavyIron and steel making
Light industrySmall, lightweight productsElectronics and healthcare products
Footloose industryIndustry that is not tied to one placeUsually light industries that locate close to cities
💡 Heavy = hard to carry, so the factory sits near the materials. Light = easy to carry, so it can sit almost anywhere (footloose = “loose feet”, free to move).
🏢 Footloose Industry and Industrial Estates
Footloose industries can locate anywhere. Previously, factories had to be near a source of coal for energy; now they are no longer tied down.
Industrial estates are sites at the edge of urban areas to be used by industries. Example: Sandyford Business Park, Dublin.
  • Cheaper land and space to expand
  • Services like water, electricity and fast broadband
  • Access to motorways
  • Can locate beside factories that supply semi-finished products
🕰️ Industrial Inertia
Industrial inertia is when an industry doesn’t relocate, even though it might make sense to do so. It is generally due to historical ties.
💡 Like staying at your old school’s sports club after you’ve moved house: the habits, equipment and friends are all still there.

Example: Sheffield steel, England.

⚠️ Common mistake: saying inertia means “the factory closes down”. It means the industry stays where it is even though moving might make more sense.

❓ Quick Quiz

Which is a secondary economic activity?

  1. Mining bauxite
  2. Farming potatoes
  3. Making crisps in a factory
  4. Fishing
Secondary activities manufacture products from raw materials. Mining, farming and fishing extract raw materials (primary).

In a crisp factory, which of these is a process?

  1. Potatoes
  2. Cooking
  3. Crisps
  4. Waste
Cutting and cooking are processes. Potatoes are inputs; crisps and waste are outputs.

Why might a factory using bulky, heavy raw materials locate close to them?

  1. To cut transportation costs
  2. To be near the market
  3. To use the IDA
  4. To find skilled labour
Moving heavy materials is costly, so being close cuts transport costs.

A bakery locating near a flour mill is an example of which factor?

  1. Market
  2. Capital
  3. Services
  4. Linkages
The bakery relies on a product made by another company: linkages.

What is skilled labour?

  1. Workers trained on the job
  2. Work that requires third-level qualifications
  3. Unpaid workers
  4. Workers in the market
Semi-skilled labour is trained on the job; skilled needs third-level qualifications.

Which body works to attract industry to Ireland?

  1. EU
  2. HSE
  3. IDA
  4. RUSAL
The IDA (Industrial Development Authority) attracts industry. Ireland’s corporation tax is 12.5%.

Iron and steel making is an example of…

  1. Heavy industry
  2. Light industry
  3. Footloose industry
  4. Primary industry
Its raw materials and products are bulky and heavy.

Which statement best describes a footloose industry?

  1. It must be next to a coal mine

Footloose industries (usually light, near cities) are no longer tied to coal or raw materials.

Which is NOT a reason to locate on an industrial estate?

✏️ Practice Questions

Written from your class notes. Click a question to reveal a model answer.

1. Explain the difference between primary and secondary economic activities.

Primary activities are the extraction of raw materials. Secondary activities take raw materials and manufacture a product.

2. Draw an inputs–processes–outputs flowchart for a named factory.

Crisp factory: Inputs (potatoes, flavouring, labour) → Processes (cutting, cooking) → Outputs (crisps, or waste).

3. Name and explain three factors that influence the location of a factory.

Raw materials: if bulky or heavy, the factory locates closer to them to cut transportation costs.

Transport: access to air, sea, road and rail links to receive materials and ship out products.

Labour: being close to towns/cities provides a workforce; universities provide skilled workers.

(Others: services, market, linkages, capital, government policy, personal preference.)

4. How does the Irish government encourage industry to locate in Ireland?

Government agencies help Irish companies set up in Ireland or abroad by giving loans and grants. The IDA works to attract industry, and Ireland has a low 12.5% corporation tax.

5. Distinguish between heavy, light and footloose industry, with an example of each.

Heavy: bulky, heavy raw materials and products, e.g. iron and steel making.

Light: small, lightweight products, e.g. electronics and healthcare products.

Footloose: not tied to one place; usually light industries that locate close to cities.

6. Why do companies locate on industrial estates? Name an example.

Sites at the edge of urban areas offer cheaper land, space to expand, services (water, electricity, fast broadband), access to motorways, and can be beside factories that supply semi-finished products. Example: Sandyford Business Park, Dublin.

7. What is industrial inertia? Use an example.

When an industry doesn’t relocate even though it might make sense to, generally due to historical ties. Example: Sheffield steel: the coal mines have closed but the steel factories remain; instead of relocating they changed approach, using new energy sources and producing new products such as surgical instruments.
