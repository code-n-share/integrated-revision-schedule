🌬️ Breathing basics
The respiratory system is the system responsible for breathing. Breathing in is called inhalation. Breathing out is called exhalation.

It has two main functions: (1) to take in oxygen from the atmosphere; (2) to give out carbon dioxide and water vapour to the atmosphere.

💡 In = inhalation (oxygen in). Out = exhalation (ex = exit, carbon dioxide and water vapour out).
🎯 Concentrate!
Don’t confuse the respiratory system with respiration, which is the way energy is released from food. To tell them apart, the respiratory system is often called “the breathing system”.
⚠️ Common mistake: writing “respiration” when the question is about breathing. Breathing = respiratory system; respiration = releasing energy from food.
🧭 Structures of the respiratory system

Follow the air from the outside in:

Mouth / nose→Pharynx + epiglottis→Trachea→2 Bronchi→Bronchioles→Alveoli
StructureWhat it does
Mouth and noseAir enters through the mouth and nose. Air entering through the nose is warmed and moistened so gases are easier to exchange between lungs and blood. Hairs and mucus in the nose clean the air by removing dust and micro-organisms (bacteria).
EpiglottisA flap of tissue that stops food going down the “wrong way” and entering the trachea.
TracheaCommonly known as the windpipe. Made from c-shaped rings of cartilage that keep it open. Allows air to flow to the bronchus.
BronchusThe trachea divides into two bronchi, one for each lung, allowing air to flow into and out of the lungs.
LungsTwo large, spongy, elastic organs in the chest cavity. They expand easily during inhalation and contract back to normal size during exhalation.
BronchiolesEach bronchus divides into increasingly narrow tubes in the lungs.
Alveolus (alveoli)At the tip of each bronchiole is a tiny air sac. There are millions in the lungs (about 300 million in each lung). This is where gases are exchanged between blood and air.
🧩 Remember it: the airway is an upside-down tree: trachea (trunk) → bronchi (big branches) → bronchioles (twigs) → alveoli (leaves).
💡 The c-shaped cartilage rings work like the rings in a vacuum-cleaner hose: they stop the tube squashing flat.
⚠️ Common mistake: mixing up the trachea (one tube, the windpipe) with the oesophagus (food pipe) – the epiglottis keeps food out of the trachea.
🔄 Gas exchange and diffusion
Diffusion allows each gas to move from an area where it is in high concentration to an area where it is in low concentration. The walls of the alveoli and capillaries are very thin (one cell thick), which allows the gases to pass through easily.
💡 Like the smell of toast spreading from the kitchen: it drifts from where there is lots of it to where there is little.
  • The alveoli are surrounded by capillaries that carry blood.
  • Oxygen moves from the alveoli → to the capillaries (into the blood).
  • Carbon dioxide and water move from the capillaries → to the alveoli (and are breathed out).
Air in alveolus: oxygen→ diffuses into →Blood in capillary
Blood in capillary: CO₂ + water→ diffuses into →Air in alveolus
🔎 Eureka: there are approximately 300 million alveoli in each lung, providing a large surface area for gas exchange.
⚠️ Common mistake: saying the lungs “make” oxygen or that oxygen is “breathed into the blood”. Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the capillaries because there is more of it in the alveoli.

🔬 Experimental Investigation 8.1: How can we test for carbon dioxide in inhaled and exhaled air?

Equipment: droppers, limewater, 2 test tubes, plastic straws, two-holed rubber bungs. Safety: do not drink the limewater.

  1. Use a dropper to put equal volumes of limewater into the two test tubes.
  2. Fit straws into the rubber bungs and the bungs into both tubes (Fig 8.5).
  3. Observe and record how clear or milky the limewater is in both tubes.
  4. Breathe in through straw A and breathe out through straw B for 3 minutes.
  5. Observe and record how clear or milky the limewater is in both tubes again.
💡 Limewater is a test liquid: the milkier it goes, the more carbon dioxide has passed through it. Comparing tube A and tube B compares inhaled and exhaled air.
⚠️ Common mistake: mixing up the straws. You breathe in through A (so air from the room is drawn through the limewater) and out through B (so your exhaled air is bubbled through it).
💪 Breathing: diaphragm and intercostal muscles
The diaphragm is a large sheet of muscle below the lungs, at the bottom of the ribcage.
  • Inhalation: the diaphragm contracts and flattens to bring air into the lungs.
  • Exhalation: it relaxes and returns to its dome shape to force air out of the lungs.
The intercostal muscles move the ribs during inhalation and exhalation.
Inhalation (breathing in)Exhalation (breathing out)
DiaphragmContracts and flattensRelaxes, returns to dome shape
LungsExpand; air is drawn in from the atmosphereContract back to normal size; air is pushed out to the atmosphere
💡 Flat when you take air in, dome when you push it out – like a trampoline pulling flat as air rushes in.
🔬 Experimental Investigation 8.2: How can we model the chest, lungs and diaphragm?

Equipment: 2-litre plastic bottle, balloons, scissors, elastic bands. Safety: be careful when using scissors.

  • Plastic bottle (top two-thirds, base cut off) = the chest
  • Balloon through the neck of the bottle = the lung
  • Cut balloon stretched over the open bottom = the diaphragm

Pinch the diaphragm balloon at the bottom and pull it down 1 cm at a time (1–5 cm), observing and recording how much the “lung” balloon inflates.

🔬 Experimental Investigation 8.3: What happens to our breathing rate when we exercise?

Equipment: timer. Safety: make sure you are medically fit to exercise.

  1. In pairs: A is active, B records and times.
  2. A rests for 2 minutes, then counts breaths for 30 seconds; B records. Repeat twice more and calculate A’s average resting breathing rate (breaths per minute).
  3. Repeat after low–moderate exercise (brisk walk, 2 minutes) and moderate–high exercise (jog/run, about three-quarter pace, 2 minutes).
  4. Swap roles and plot the data on a suitable chart or graph.
⚠️ Common mistake: saying the diaphragm “relaxes” during inhalation. It is the opposite: contracts and flattens to breathe in, relaxes to breathe out.
🤝 How the body’s systems work together

The digestive, circulatory and respiratory systems work together to carry out tasks that no one system could do on its own. This makes the human body very well organised.

SystemsHow they work together
Digestive + circulatoryFood is broken down into forms that can be absorbed into the bloodstream (e.g. starch → maltose → glucose). Capillaries in the small intestine absorb the nutrients into the blood. The blood delivers the nutrients to all the cells for energy and growth.
Digestive + respiratoryThe pharynx (throat) is the opening for both: food enters the oesophagus and air enters the trachea. The epiglottis stops food going down the wrong way and blocking the trachea. Mucus in the nose traps dust and micro-organisms; micro-organisms in swallowed mucus are killed by the acid in the stomach.
Circulatory + respiratoryThe pulmonary artery takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs. Diffusion allows the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide at the alveoli. Capillaries containing oxygenated blood join to form the pulmonary vein, which carries oxygenated blood to the left atrium of the heart.
⚠️ Common mistake: describing one system only. “Work together” answers need a link between two systems (e.g. blood vessels collecting oxygen at the alveoli).

1. Copy and complete: During inhalation, the lungs ______. Air is drawn ______ from the atmosphere. During exhalation, the lungs ______. Air is pushed ______ to the atmosphere. (Summary Q1)

During inhalation, the lungs expand. Air is drawn in from the atmosphere. During exhalation, the lungs contract (back to normal size). Air is pushed out to the atmosphere.

3. Fig 8.10 shows a model of the respiratory system (bell jar with a pink balloon at the bottom, a Y-shaped tube and two yellow balloons inside). Identify the part represented by: (a) the yellow balloons (b) the Y-shaped tube (c) the bell jar (d) the pink balloon. (Summary Q5; Experiment 8.2)

Based on the model in Experiment 8.2 (bottle = chest, balloon = lung, cut balloon = diaphragm); check (b) against your class notes.

4. (a) Explain the term “diffusion”. (b) Give one example of diffusion in the respiratory system, identifying the gas and location. (c) Outline two ways alveoli are suited to their job. (Summary Q6–7) [5 marks]

(a) Diffusion is the movement of a gas from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration.

(b) Oxygen moves from the alveoli (high concentration) into the capillaries (low concentration). Or: carbon dioxide moves from the capillaries into the alveoli.

(c) There are millions of alveoli (about 300 million per lung), giving a large surface area for gas exchange; the walls of the alveoli and the capillaries around them are very thin (one cell thick), so gases pass through easily.

5. The percentage of carbon dioxide in a person’s exhaled air at rest is 4.1%, after low–moderate activity 6.8%, and after moderate–high activity 9.3%. (a) What trend can you see? (b) Explain why there is more carbon dioxide in exhaled air than inhaled air. (Summary Q8)

(a) The percentage of carbon dioxide in exhaled air increases as the level of physical activity increases (4.1% → 6.8% → 9.3%).

(b) Gases are exchanged at the alveoli: carbon dioxide moves by diffusion from the capillaries into the alveoli and is breathed out, so exhaled air (4%) has more carbon dioxide than inhaled air (0.04%).

The textbook pages we have don’t give the reason for the trend – add it from your class notes.

6. Final Examination 2019, Q9 (Fig 8.11). A points to the nose/mouth region, B points to the tube leading to the lungs, C points to a lung. (a) Match words (Lung, Trachea, Liver, Oesophagus, Mouth) to the letters. (b) Describe what happens in the respiratory system when a person breathes in.

(a) A = Mouth, B = Trachea, C = Lung.

(b) Air enters through the mouth (and nose); the diaphragm contracts and flattens and the intercostal muscles move the ribs; air passes down the trachea and bronchi into the lungs, which expand; oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the capillaries.

lungs, stomach, small intestine, heart, veins

8. Give one example of how the following systems work together: (a) circulatory and respiratory (b) respiratory and digestive (c) digestive and circulatory. (Summary Q9)
  • (a) The pulmonary artery takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs, where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged by diffusion at the alveoli; the pulmonary vein returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium.
  • (b) The pharynx is the opening for both systems; the epiglottis stops food blocking the trachea. Or: mucus traps micro-organisms, which are killed by stomach acid when swallowed.
  • (c) Food is broken down (starch → maltose → glucose); capillaries in the small intestine absorb the nutrients into the blood, which delivers them to all cells for energy and growth.
