🌍 The Earth, Sun and Moon
Earth’s distance from the Sun is what makes life possible on our planet. Earth is close enough to benefit from the Sun’s light and heat energy and far enough away to avoid extreme temperatures. The Moon also helps support life by keeping the climate balanced.

Shadows, day and night, seasons, phases of the Moon and eclipses are all caused by how the Earth, Sun and Moon interact with each other.

MovementTime taken
The Earth spins once on its axis24 hours
The Moon orbits the Earth27 days
The Earth orbits the Sun365.25 days
🧩 Remember it: the three numbers in order of size: 24 hours (spin), 27 days (Moon orbit), 365.25 days (Earth orbit).
🌳 Shadows
When rays of light from the Sun reach the Earth, they cause shadows to form. This happens because:
  • light travels in straight lines
  • light cannot pass through an opaque object (an object that blocks light)
The position of the Sun in the sky affects how long the shadow formed by an object will be.
💡 Like standing in front of a torch in a dark room: the light can’t bend round you, so there is a dark patch behind you.

🌗 Days
Earth’s axis is an imaginary line that links the North and South poles. The Earth is tilted at an angle of 23.5° on its axis. When the Earth spins, half the planet faces towards the Sun (in the light) and half faces away from the Sun (in the dark). This causes day and night. The Earth takes 24 hours to complete one full spin on its axis.
💡 Think of a spinning globe with a torch shining on it: the side facing the torch is day, the side facing away is night, and as it turns every place gets both.
⚠️ Common mistake: saying the Sun moves round the Earth to make day and night. It is the Earth spinning on its axis (once in 24 hours).
🍂 Seasons
The gravitational force of the Sun pulls the Earth into an elliptical (oval) orbit. Each orbit takes 365.25 days. A season is a period of the year when the weather and the number of hours of daylight change. Seasons change on or around 21 March, June, September and December.

Seasons on Earth occur for two reasons:

  1. The time taken for the Earth to travel around the Sun (365.25 days).
  2. The angle of the Earth’s tilt on its axis stays the same during its orbit of the Sun (23.5°).
  • Summer: the tilt means Ireland spends more time in daylight than in the shadows.
  • Winter: the nights in Ireland are longer than the days due to the tilt of the Earth’s axis.
  • The seasons in the northern and southern hemispheres are opposite.
🧩 Remember it: seasons are caused by the tilt, not by Earth being nearer to or further from the Sun. The tilt always points the same way, so as Earth orbits, first one hemisphere and then the other leans towards the Sun.
⚠️ Common mistake: “it is summer because the Earth is closer to the Sun.” Wrong: it is the tilt of the axis (23.5°) as the Earth orbits the Sun.

🎯 Model answer: “Explain why there are temperature differences between summer and winter” [4 marks]
  1. The Earth’s axis is tilted at 23.5° and stays tilted the same way as the Earth orbits the Sun.
  2. In summer Ireland is tilted towards the Sun, so the Sun’s rays hit at a higher angle and there are longer days.
  3. The heat energy is spread over a smaller area, so it is warmer.
  4. In winter it is the opposite: tilted away, rays hit at a lower angle, shorter days, heat spread over a larger area, cooler.

🌙 The lunar cycle
The Moon does not emit its own light. It can be seen from Earth because light from the Sun reflects off its surface.

The Moon orbits the Earth every 27 days and also rotates on its own axis every 27 days. So the same side of the Moon always faces Earth. The side we cannot see is called the ‘far side’ or the ‘dark side’ of the Moon.

The Moon doesn’t change shape. As it orbits the Earth, the amount of reflected light we can see changes. These changes are the lunar phases, and they repeat as a lunar cycle.

#PhaseWhat we see from Earth
1New moonMoon is lined up with the Earth and the Sun; the side facing Earth is not lit by the Sun, so it cannot be seen.
2Waxing crescentA small part of the side facing Earth is lit and starts to become visible.
3First quarterHalf of the side facing Earth is lit and can now be seen.
4Waxing gibbousMore than half of the side facing Earth is visible, as it is lit by the Sun.
5Full moonMoon is lined up with the Earth and Sun again; all of the side facing Earth is lit, so we see a full disc.
6Waning gibbousMoon appears to grow smaller each night, as less of the side facing Earth is lit by the Sun.
7Third quarterOnly half of the side that can be seen from Earth is lit by the Sun.
8Waning crescentMoon appears as a crescent again and keeps waning until none of the side facing Earth is lit and the cycle begins again with a new moon.
Waxing = getting bigger · Waning = getting smallerA full lunar cycle (new moon to new moon) takes approximately one month (29.5 days)
🧩 Remember it: New → Waxing Crescent → First quarter → Waxing Gibbous → Full → Waning Gibbous → Third quarter → Waning Crescent. Crescent = a sliver; gibbous = more than half but not full.
⚠️ Common mistake: mixing up 27 days (the Moon orbits the Earth once) with 29.5 days (new moon to new moon, the lunar cycle). Use the number that matches the question.
⚠️ Common mistake: saying the Earth’s shadow makes the phases. Phases happen because we see different amounts of the Moon’s lit side; the Earth’s shadow is only involved in a lunar eclipse.
🌑 Eclipses
An eclipse occurs when the Sun’s light is blocked by either the Moon or the Earth.
Solar eclipseLunar eclipse
What happensThe Moon moves between the Sun and the EarthThe Earth moves between the Sun and the Moon
ShadowThe Moon’s shadow is cast onto the surface of the EarthThe Earth’s shadow is cast onto the surface of the Moon
Order in lineSun – Moon – EarthSun – Earth – Moon
  • The umbra is the darkest part of the shadow: the light from the Sun is completely blocked by the Moon or Earth.
  • The penumbra is where the light from the Sun is only partially blocked.
  • Eclipses do not happen every month because the Earth, Moon and Sun do not always line up exactly. Lunar eclipses are more common than solar eclipses.
🔎 Eureka: ancient Greeks were able to work out the diameter of the Earth using data they gathered from lunar eclipses.
🧩 Remember it: the name tells you what gets covered: in a solar eclipse the Sun is covered by the Moon (Moon in the middle); in a lunar eclipse the Moon is covered by Earth’s shadow (Earth in the middle).
⚠️ Safety (and an exam point): the Sun’s UV radiation can burn the retinas in our eyes, leading to permanent damage or even blindness. Never look directly at the Sun. Always wear special eclipse glasses or use a pinhole camera to view an eclipse.

✏️ Practice Questions

Questions are from the textbook (Test tube, Summary questions and past papers). Click a question to reveal a model answer. I wrote them from the textbook text, so check them against the book’s answer key. Where a diagram or photo had to be read by eye, the answer says so.

1. What does the term ‘axis’ mean? (Summary Q1) [2 marks]

The axis is an imaginary line that links the North and South poles, about which the Earth spins. It is tilted at 23.5°.

2. Explain how shadows form on a sunny day. (Summary Q2) [3 marks]

Rays of light from the Sun travel in straight lines. They cannot pass through an opaque object (one that blocks light), so a dark area, the shadow, forms behind the object. The position of the Sun in the sky affects the length of the shadow.

3. Explain why one day on Earth is 24 hours, and use a diagram to explain how night and day occur. (Test tube Q3–4)

The Earth takes 24 hours to complete one full spin on its axis, which is one day.

Diagram: draw the Earth with rays from the Sun hitting one half. The half facing the Sun is in daylight; the half facing away is in darkness (night). As the Earth spins, places move from day to night and back.

4. What causes the seasons on Earth to occur? (Summary Q3) [3 marks]

(1) The Earth takes 365.25 days to travel around the Sun. (2) The Earth’s axis is tilted at 23.5°, and this tilt stays the same during its orbit, so different parts of the Earth are tilted towards or away from the Sun at different times of year.

5. What position is Ireland in, in relation to the Sun, when it experiences (a) winter (b) summer? (Summary Q4) [2 marks]

(a) Winter: Ireland is tilted away from the Sun.

(b) Summer: Ireland is tilted towards the Sun.

6. The shadows created during the summer and the winter differ from each other. Explain this difference using diagrams. (Summary Q5) [4 marks]

Summer: the Sun is high in the sky, its rays hit the ground at a higher angle and cover a smaller area, so shadows are shorter.

Winter: the Sun is low in the sky, its rays hit at a lower angle and spread over a larger area, so shadows are longer.

Draw it like Fig 36.8: the Sun high with steep rays, and the Sun low with slanted rays. The book gives the rule that the Sun’s position affects shadow length; “shorter in summer, longer in winter” follows from the Sun’s height.

7. Copy and complete using “27 days”, “24 hours”, “365.25 days”: The Moon orbits the Earth once every ______. The Earth spins on its own axis once every ______. The Earth orbits the Sun once every ______. (Summary Q6)

27 days; 24 hours; 365.25 days

8. (a) How would the Earth’s seasons differ if the Earth were not tilted? (Summary Q7) (b) Explain why there are temperature differences between summer and winter on Earth. (Test tube Q9) [5 marks]

(a) The seasons are caused by the tilt staying the same as the Earth orbits the Sun. Without the tilt there would be no tilt-based seasons: the Sun’s rays would hit each place at the same angle all year, with similar days and nights.

(b) In summer the part of the Earth is tilted towards the Sun: rays hit at a higher angle, longer days, heat spread over a smaller area, warmer. In winter it is tilted away: lower angle, shorter days, heat spread over a larger area, cooler.

(a) is a reasoned answer from the “two reasons” rule; the book doesn’t spell it out. Check with your class notes.

9. Use Fig 36.6 to work out what season it is at the North Pole when it has (a) daylight for 24 hours (b) night-time for 24 hours. Also: when does winter begin in the southern hemisphere, if winter begins in December in the northern hemisphere? (Test tube Q6, Q10)

(a) Summer: the North Pole is tilted towards the Sun, so it stays in daylight.

(b) Winter: the North Pole is tilted away from the Sun, so it stays in darkness.

Southern hemisphere winter begins in June, because seasons in the two hemispheres are opposite.

These are worked out from Fig 36.6 and the “seasons are opposite” rule; check with your key.

10. Explain the difference between (a) waxing and waning (b) new moon and full moon (c) crescent moon and gibbous moon. (Summary Q8) [6 marks]

(a) Waxing = getting bigger (more of the lit side visible each night); waning = getting smaller.

(b) New moon: the side facing Earth is not lit, so it cannot be seen. Full moon: all of the side facing Earth is lit, so we see a full disc.

(c) Crescent: only a small part (a sliver) is lit. Gibbous: more than half, but not all, of the side facing Earth is lit.

11. Explain why we only ever see the same side of the Moon from Earth, and why we cannot see a new moon. (Test tube Q11–12) [4 marks]

The Moon rotates on its own axis every 27 days, the same time it takes to orbit the Earth, so the same side always faces us (the other side is the far or ‘dark’ side).

At new moon the Moon is lined up with the Earth and Sun, and the side facing Earth is not lit by the Sun, so it cannot be seen.

  • (a) New moon: a completely dark circle (or just an outline).
  • (b) Waxing crescent: a small sliver lit on the right edge (as seen from Ireland).
  • (c) Full moon: the whole circle lit.
  • (d) Waning crescent: a small sliver lit on the left edge.

Left/right sides are for the northern hemisphere, taken from the book’s photos (Fig 36.11 waxing crescent lit on the right; Fig 36.13 third quarter lit on the left).

13. Explain how (a) a solar eclipse occurs (b) a lunar eclipse occurs. (Summary Q10–11) [4 marks]

(a) A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon moves between the Sun and the Earth. The Moon’s shadow is cast onto the surface of the Earth.

(b) A lunar eclipse occurs when the Earth moves between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth’s shadow is cast onto the surface of the Moon.

They do not happen every month because the Earth, Moon and Sun do not always line up exactly.

(d) The surface temperature rises and falls in a regular repeating pattern over time (about 30 days per cycle). A reasonable explanation: as the Moon rotates, a given area of the surface faces the Sun and heats up, then faces away and cools.
