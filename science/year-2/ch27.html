🎢 Motion
When an object moves, it is said to be in motion. Movement in a straight line is called linear motion.
Type of motionExample
Moving in a straight line (linear)A car on a road
Moving in a circleA Ferris wheel
Changing directionA rollercoaster
💡 “Linear” = “line”. If you plot linear motion at constant speed on a graph you always get a straight (linear) line.
🏃 Speed
Speed describes how fast an object in motion travels from one position to another. The speed of an object is the distance it travels divided by the time taken.
speed (m/s) = distance (m) ÷ time (s)Unit of speed in science: m/s (metres per second)
💡 A speedometer-style way to think of it: speed tells you “how many metres per second”. 5 m/s means 5 metres every second.
🧩 Remember it – the formula triangle: put distance on top and speed × time on the bottom. Cover the quantity you want and use what you can still see:
distance = speed × timetime = distance ÷ speed
  • Cover distance → speed × time
  • Cover speed → distance ÷ time
  • Cover time → distance ÷ speed
EventSpeed (m/s)
Walking1.5
Sprinting8
Car at national speed limit27.7
Passenger plane250
Speed of sound330
Earth moving around the Sun30 000
Speed of light in a vacuum300 000 000
🔎 Eureka: the speed of light is the fastest thing in the universe. Light takes 1.3 s to travel from the Moon to Earth, a distance of nearly 390 000 km.
⚠️ Common mistake: units. Speed in everyday life is given in km/h (the national speed limit on primary roads is 100 km/h, just less than 28 m/s). If a question gives km or hours, convert to metres (m) and seconds (s) before calculating speed. Minutes → × 60 to get seconds.
📝 Worked example: a train at a constant 30 m/s

(a) How long does it take to travel 2.1 km?

time = distance ÷ speed = 2100 ÷ 30rearrange
time = 70 s (1 minute 10 seconds)answer

(b) How far does it travel in 10 minutes?

10 minutes = 10 × 60 = 600 sconvert units
distance = speed × time = 30 × 600rearrange
distance = 18 000 m (18 km)answer

🧭 Velocity
Velocity describes the speed of an object in motion and the direction it travels in. For example, a car travelling at a speed of 20 m/s east has a velocity of 20 m/s east.
velocity = distance (m) ÷ time (s), plus a directionSame unit as speed (m/s), but always state the direction
🧩 Remember it: velocity = speed + direction. Speed says “how fast?”; velocity says “how fast and which way?”.
📝 Worked example: the cyclist

A cyclist lives 3 km north of his school. It takes him 5 minutes to travel there. What is his velocity?

velocity = 3000 m ÷ (5 × 60 s)convert units
velocity = 3000 m ÷ 300 sdivide
velocity = 10 m/s southadd direction

As the cyclist lives north of his school, he must travel south to get there.

⚠️ Common mistake: leaving out the direction. A velocity answer needs a number, the unit and a direction (e.g. 80 m/s to the left).
🚗 Acceleration
Acceleration is the rate at which the velocity of an object changes. It is the difference between the initial and final velocity: final velocity minus initial velocity.
💡 An object speeding up or slowing down is accelerating. The accelerator pedal makes a car increase in velocity every second; the brake pedal makes it decrease in velocity every second.
acceleration (m/s²) = change in velocity (m/s) ÷ time taken (s)change in velocity = final velocity − initial velocity · Unit: m/s² (metres per second squared)
📝 Worked example 1: speeding up

A car is moving at 10 m/s and changes its velocity to 20 m/s in 5 s. Calculate its acceleration.

change in velocity = 20 − 10 = 10 m/sfinal − initial
acceleration = 10 ÷ 5÷ time
acceleration = 2 m/s²answer
📝 Worked example 2: slowing down

A motorbike is travelling at 24 m/s, then brakes to a stop in 3 s. Calculate its acceleration.

change in velocity = 0 − 24 = −24 m/sfinal − initial
acceleration = −24 ÷ 3÷ time
acceleration = −8 m/s²negative = slowing

The motorbike has a negative acceleration because it slowed down, i.e. its velocity decreased.

⚠️ Common mistake: doing initial − final. Always final minus initial, so slowing down gives a negative answer. Also: the unit is m/s², not m/s.
📈 Distance-time graphs
A distance-time graph can be used to calculate an object’s speed. It shows distance on the y-axis and time on the x-axis.
Shape of lineWhat it means
Straight line sloping upwardsConstant speed. To find it, take any point and divide the distance value by the time value.
Straight line, less steepMoving more slowly at a constant speed.
Curved line getting steeperSpeed is changing: the object is getting faster. Take any two points and divide the distance values by the time values.
Horizontal lineThe object has stopped (speed 0 m/s).
🧩 Remember it: on a distance-time graph, steeper = faster, flat = stopped.

  • Fig 27.10: line A reaches 16 m in 4 s → 4 m/s; line B reaches 24 m in 4 s → 6 m/s. B is steeper, so B is moving faster.

📝 Extra practice (not from the textbook): a journey in stages

A student runs 60 m away from the start in 20 s (P→Q), stops for 10 s (Q→R), then runs back to the start in 30 s (R→S). Find the speed in each stage and the average speed for the whole journey.

P→Q: 60 ÷ 20 = 3 m/ssteep, away
Q→R: 0 ÷ 10 = 0 m/shorizontal = stopped
R→S: 60 ÷ 30 = 2 m/sback to start
whole journey: (60 + 60) ÷ 60 s = 2 m/stotal distance ÷ total time

R→S is less steep than P→Q, so the run back was slower. The total distance run is 120 m, even though the student ends up where they started.

⚠️ Common mistake: saying a horizontal line on a distance-time graph means “constant speed”. It means the object has stopped: the distance isn’t changing.
📉 Velocity-time graphs
A velocity-time graph can be used to calculate an object’s acceleration. It looks similar to a distance-time graph, except it shows velocity on the y-axis. To calculate acceleration between two points, divide the change in velocity by the time taken.
Shape of lineWhat it meansExample from the graph above
Straight line sloping upConstant positive acceleration; speeding upA→B: 4 m/s in 4 s = 1 m/s²
Horizontal lineAcceleration is zero; velocity is constantB→C: 0 m/s in 3 s = 0 m/s²
Steeper slopeGreater accelerationC→D: 3 m/s in 1 s = 3 m/s²
Straight line sloping downConstant negative acceleration; slowing downD→E: −7 m/s in 5 s = −1.4 m/s²
📝 Extra practice (not from the textbook): acceleration from a graph

Find the acceleration in each stage of the graph.

P→Q: (10 − 2) ÷ 4 = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 m/s²sloping up
Q→R: (10 − 10) ÷ 3 = 0 m/s²horizontal
R→S: (2 − 10) ÷ 4 = −8 ÷ 4 = −2 m/s²negative = slowing
🧩 Remember it: on a velocity-time graph, slope = acceleration, flat = constant velocity, down = slowing.
⚠️ Common mistake: mixing up the two graphs. A flat line on a distance-time graph = stopped. A flat line on a velocity-time graph = moving at constant velocity (acceleration 0). Always check the y-axis label first.
🔬 Investigating motion
🔬 Experimental Investigation 27.1: How can we measure the speed of an object?

Equipment: trundle wheel, tape (or chalk), stopwatch. Safety: make sure the area is clear; wear appropriate clothing and shoes; do not run too fast.

  1. Use a trundle wheel to mark out a straight line. Place tape (or a chalk mark) every 5 m, up to 35 m in total.
  2. One student walks along the line while another records their time with a stopwatch at each 5 m mark, until the end of the line.
  3. Record the distance and time in a table. Use the total distance and time to calculate the average speed.
  4. Repeat with different students moving at different speeds and record in the table.
  5. Compare each student’s overall average speed to their speeds at each marking.
  6. Draw a distance-time graph to represent the data.
  7. Compare the slope of each student’s graph to their average speed.
🔬 Experimental Investigation 27.2: How can we measure the acceleration of an object?

Equipment: track, books, protractor, motion sensor, data logger, laptop, trolley.

  1. Place one side of the track on a stack of books. Use the protractor to raise it to an angle of 10° from horizontal.
  2. Connect the motion sensor to the data logger and laptop. Place the motion sensor at the top of the track facing downwards.
  3. Hold the trolley at the top of the track and start recording with the sensor.
  4. Release the trolley until it reaches the end of the track (have a student ready to catch it).
  5. Record the speed at the start and end, and the time taken, in a table.
  6. Repeat, raising the track another 5° each time until you reach 35°, using the protractor to measure the angle.
  7. Compare the speed changes and accelerations calculated for each angle.
  8. Draw a graph of speed against time for each angle (velocity on the y-axis in m/s, time on the x-axis in s).
💡 Measuring the start and end speed and the time lets you use acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time.
⚠️ Common mistake: in Investigation 27.2 only the angle of the track changes. Keep the same trolley and track so the comparison is fair.

❓ Quick Quiz

What is the unit of speed in science?

  1. km/h
  2. m/s²
  3. m/s
  4. m
Speed is measured in m/s (metres per second). m/s² is the unit of acceleration; km/h is used in everyday life but must be converted for calculations.

What is the difference between speed and velocity?

  1. Velocity is slower than speed
  2. Velocity includes the direction of travel; speed does not
  3. Speed includes the direction of travel; velocity does not
  4. Speed is measured in m/s and velocity in m/s²
Velocity is speed in a given direction (e.g. 20 m/s east). Both are measured in m/s.

A train moves at a constant 30 m/s. How far does it travel in 10 minutes?

  1. 18 000 m
  2. 300 m
  3. 3 m
  4. 180 m
Convert first: 10 min = 600 s. Distance = 30 × 600 = 18 000 m. 300 m comes from using 10 instead of 600 s; 3 m from dividing 30 by 10.

A train moves at a constant 30 m/s. How long does it take to travel 2.1 km?

  1. 0.07 s
  2. 63 000 s
  3. 14.3 s
  4. 70 s
2.1 km = 2100 m. Time = 2100 ÷ 30 = 70 s. 0.07 s comes from forgetting to convert km to m; 63 000 s from multiplying instead of dividing.

A car’s velocity changes from 10 m/s to 20 m/s in 5 s. What is its acceleration?

  1. 6 m/s²
  2. 2 m/s²
  3. 10 m/s²
  4. 50 m/s²
Change in velocity = 20 − 10 = 10 m/s; 10 ÷ 5 = 2 m/s². 6 m/s² comes from adding the velocities (30 ÷ 5); 50 from multiplying 10 × 5.

A motorbike travelling at 24 m/s brakes to a stop in 3 s. What is its acceleration?

  1. 8 m/s²
  2. −72 m/s²
  3. −8 m/s²
  4. 0 m/s²
Change in velocity = 0 − 24 = −24 m/s; −24 ÷ 3 = −8 m/s². The negative sign shows it is slowing down. +8 forgets the sign.

What does a horizontal line on a distance-time graph show?

  1. The object has stopped
  2. The object is moving at constant speed
  3. The object is speeding up
  4. The object is moving at constant acceleration
A horizontal line means the distance is not changing, so the object has stopped (speed 0 m/s).

A distance-time graph is a curved line that gets steeper. What does this show?

  1. The object is stopped
  2. The object is moving at a constant speed
  3. The object is slowing down
  4. The object is speeding up
A curved line getting steeper means the speed is changing: the object is getting faster (e.g. 2 m/s then 3 m/s).

What does a horizontal line on a velocity-time graph show?

  1. The object has stopped
  2. Constant velocity and zero acceleration
  3. Constant positive acceleration
  4. Constant negative acceleration
A horizontal line on a velocity-time graph means the velocity is constant, so the acceleration is 0 m/s². (A horizontal line on a distance-time graph is the one that means “stopped”.)

What does a straight line sloping downwards on a velocity-time graph show?

  1. The object is moving at constant speed
  2. The object is moving backwards at a constant speed
  3. Constant negative acceleration: the object is slowing down
  4. Zero acceleration
Downward slope = velocity decreasing = negative acceleration (e.g. −1.4 m/s² from D to E).

A cyclist travels 2000 m in 6 minutes. Which gives the correct average speed?

  1. 2000 ÷ 360 = 5.6 m/s
  2. 2000 ÷ 6 = 333 m/s
  3. 2000 × 6 = 12 000 m/s
  4. 6 ÷ 2000 = 0.003 m/s
Convert 6 minutes to 360 s first, then divide distance by time: ≈ 5.6 m/s. 333 forgets to convert minutes; the others invert or multiply.

4. A vehicle on a motorway travels 1900 m in 60 s. Calculate (a) its average speed in m/s (b) how far it would travel at this speed in 2 minutes (c) how long it would take to travel 2 km. (Figure it out p.263)

(a) 1900 ÷ 60 ≈ 31.7 m/s

(b) 2 minutes = 120 s; 31.7 × 120 = 3800 m (exactly 1900 × 2)

(c) 2 km = 2000 m; 2000 ÷ 31.7 ≈ 63 s

5. A tennis ball is hit to the left and travels 40 m in 0.5 s. Calculate its average velocity. (Figure it out p.266 Q1)

velocity = 40 ÷ 0.5 = 80 m/s to the left.

6. A student leaves home at 8.20 a.m. and travels 4.2 km to school by bicycle, arriving at 8.55 a.m. They walk 400 m while in school and cycle home the same way in the evening. Calculate (a) the speed of their journey in the morning (b) the total distance travelled during the day. (Summary Q3) [4 marks]

(a) Time = 8.20 to 8.55 = 35 min = 2100 s; distance = 4200 m; speed = 4200 ÷ 2100 = 2 m/s.

(b) 4.2 km (to school) + 0.4 km (walking in school) + 4.2 km (home) = 8.8 km (8800 m).

(b) assumes the 400 m walk counts as part of the day’s total distance; check with the book’s key.

7. A motorbike leaves town A at 11.00 a.m. and arrives in town B at 12.30 p.m. Town B is 120 km west of town A. Calculate the average velocity of the motorbike. (Summary Q4) [3 marks]

Time = 1.5 h = 5400 s; distance = 120 km = 120 000 m. Velocity = 120 000 ÷ 5400 ≈ 22.2 m/s west (= 80 km/h west).

8. A stone drops off a building and hits the ground 6 seconds later with a velocity of 55 m/s. Calculate the acceleration of the stone. (Summary Q5) [3 marks]

The stone starts at rest, so the change in velocity = 55 − 0 = 55 m/s. Acceleration = 55 ÷ 6 ≈ 9.2 m/s² (downwards).

9. (a) A car moves from rest to a velocity of 25 m/s in 10 s. Calculate its acceleration. (b) An airplane brakes as soon as it lands and slows from 80 m/s to 20 m/s in 15 s. Calculate its acceleration. (Figure it out p.269)

(a) (25 − 0) ÷ 10 = 2.5 m/s²

(b) (20 − 80) ÷ 15 = −60 ÷ 15 = −4 m/s² (negative because it is slowing down)

10. Draw a distance-time graph for: time 0, 10, 20, 25, 30 s; distance 0, 12, 24, 30, 36 m. Then calculate the average speed at any point. (Summary Q6) [4 marks]

Plot time (s) on the x-axis and distance (m) on the y-axis and join the points: they form a straight line.

Speed = distance ÷ time = 12 ÷ 10 = 24 ÷ 20 = 30 ÷ 25 = 36 ÷ 30 = 1.2 m/s (the same at every point, so constant speed).

14. Final Examination 2019, Q7 (Fig 27.21): a cyclist’s distance-time graph. 16. Extra practice (not from the textbook). (a) Convert 90 km/h to m/s. (b) A student runs 60 m in 20 s, stops for 10 s, then runs 60 m back in 30 s. State the speed in each stage and the average speed for the whole journey. [5 marks]

(a) 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25 m/s (90 000 m ÷ 3600 s).

(b) Out: 60 ÷ 20 = 3 m/s. Stopped: 0 m/s. Back: 60 ÷ 30 = 2 m/s. Whole journey: total distance 120 m ÷ total time 60 s = 2 m/s.

17. Extra practice (not from the textbook). A velocity-time graph passes through (0 s, 2 m/s), (4 s, 10 m/s), (7 s, 10 m/s) and (11 s, 2 m/s). Calculate the acceleration in each stage and describe the motion. [6 marks]

0–4 s: (10 − 2) ÷ 4 = 2 m/s² (speeding up).

4–7 s: 0 m/s² (constant velocity of 10 m/s).

7–11 s: (2 − 10) ÷ 4 = −2 m/s² (slowing down).
