🔁 What the circulatory system does
The circulatory system has three main functions:
  1. To transport the substances needed for energy and growth to the body’s cells and remove waste
  2. To protect the body against disease
  3. To help regulate body temperature

It is made up of: blood, blood vessels and the heart.

💡 Think of a delivery network: the blood is the lorries, the vessels are the roads and the heart is the engine room that keeps them moving.
🧩 Remember it: T-P-R – Transport, Protect, Regulate.
⚠️ Common mistake: forgetting the temperature job. The human body must stay at around 37 °C and the movement of heat around the circulatory system helps with this.
🩸 Blood
Blood is a tissue made up of four components: plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. In a blood sample (Fig 7.1): plasma is 55%, red blood cells 44%, white blood cells and platelets 1%.
ComponentWhat it does
PlasmaA light yellow liquid, largely made up of water. Dissolved substances, including oxygen and glucose, are carried around the body in plasma. It also carries waste products, such as carbon dioxide and urea, away from the cells. Plasma transfers heat around the body.
Red blood cellsTransport oxygen to cells so that respiration can take place. They contain a red pigment called haemoglobin, which contains iron and picks up oxygen for transport.
White blood cellsProtect the body from disease. They fight harmful micro-organisms (e.g. viruses and bacteria), or produce antibodies that kill them.
PlateletsTiny fragments of larger cells. Help blood to clot: prevent blood loss and heal wounds, maintain blood pressure, and keep out micro-organisms that may cause disease.
💡 Plasma is the river; red cells are the oxygen boats; white cells are the police; platelets are the repair crew that plug leaks.
🧩 Remember it: Red = Rides oxygen; White = Wards off disease; Platelets = Plug wounds (clot).
⚠️ Common mistake: saying blood is red because of all its cells. Blood contains both red and white cells but is red in appearance because of the red pigment haemoglobin in the red blood cells.
🛣️ Blood vessels

Blood flows from and returns to the heart through the blood vessels. There are three types.

ArteriesVeinsCapillaries
DirectionCarry blood away from the heartCarry blood to the heartLink arteries and veins
WallsThick muscle walls to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped out of the heartThinner walls than arteriesOnly one cell thick, with lots of pores
OtherUsually carry oxygenated blood (high oxygen, low carbon dioxide)Lower blood pressure, so they have valves to prevent backflow. Usually carry deoxygenated blood (low oxygen, high carbon dioxide)Pores let molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass in and out of the surrounding cells
🧩 Remember it: Arteries = Away from the heart. Veins = Visit (come back to) the heart.
🎯 Concentrate!
In diagrams, oxygenated blood is shown as red and deoxygenated blood as blue. In reality blood with less oxygen is still red, just not quite as bright as blood with more oxygen.
💡 The pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein are the exceptions to “arteries carry oxygenated blood” – see the heart topic.
⚠️ Common mistake: “arteries carry oxygenated blood, veins deoxygenated” is only usually true. The defining rule is direction: arteries away from the heart, veins to it.
🫀 The heart
  • The heart acts as a pump to drive blood all over the body. It is in the upper chest, between the lungs, and is protected by the ribcage.
  • It is made of very strong cardiac muscle that can continuously pump blood.
  • It is a double pump, separated into a left side and a right side by the septum.
  • There are four chambers: the upper chambers are the left and right atria, the lower chambers are the left and right ventricles. Atria and ventricles are separated by valves.

🧩 Remember it: Atria = Above (the upper chambers); Ventricles = the lower chambers (V points down).
💡 A double pump is two pumps side by side in one heart: the right side sends blood to the lungs, the left side sends it to the body.
⚠️ Common mistake: mixing atria and ventricles. Upper = atria, lower = ventricles.
🔎 Eureka: Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288), an Arab physician born in Syria, was the first to describe the blood vessels in the heart and how blood flows between the heart and the lungs (pulmonary circulation).
➡️ How the heart pumps blood

One complete cycle through the body has eight stages:

  1. Deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium through the vena cava.
  2. Blood is pumped from the right atrium through a valve into the right ventricle.
  3. The right ventricle contracts and forces blood through a different valve into the pulmonary artery.
  4. The pulmonary artery delivers blood to the lungs: carbon dioxide is lost and oxygen is taken into the blood.
  5. The pulmonary vein brings oxygenated blood back to the left atrium.
  6. Blood is pumped from the left atrium through a valve into the left ventricle.
  7. The left ventricle contracts and forces blood through a different valve into the aorta, which delivers it to all cells and organs through other arteries.
  8. Exchange of molecules occurs at the capillaries. Capillaries join to form veins; the largest veins, the venae cavae, return to the right atrium. One cycle complete.
Vena cava→Right atrium→Right ventricle→Pulmonary artery→Lungs (capillaries)→Pulmonary vein→Left atrium→Left ventricle→Aorta→Body (capillaries)→Vena cava

Blue = deoxygenated, red = oxygenated.

🧩 Remember it: right side = deoxygenated blood to the lungs; left side = oxygenated blood to the body. Order of the chambers: RA → RV → lungs → LA → LV → body.
🎯 Concentrate!
The pulmonary artery is the only artery that carries deoxygenated blood. The pulmonary vein is the only vein that carries oxygenated blood.
⚠️ Common mistake: sending oxygenated blood to the lungs. Blood goes to the lungs deoxygenated and comes back oxygenated.
💓 Heartbeat and pulse
Heartbeat: doctors listen with a stethoscope. The lub-dub sound is made when the valves in the heart close after blood passes through. The normal adult heart rate at rest is between 60 and 100 beats per minute.
Pulse: a wave of vibration that passes through an artery when blood is forced out of the heart. It can be felt at an artery close to the skin, such as at the wrist. The pulse rate and heart rate are the same, so the pulse tells us if the heart is beating at a normal rate.
💡 Feel your wrist: each “tap” is one heartbeat sending a wave along the artery.

A person’s age, sex, physical fitness, diet and stress affect their heart rate. A balanced diet, exercise and rest are very important for a healthy heart.

🔬 Experimental Investigation 7.1: How does exercise affect pulse rate?

Equipment: timer; pulse rate monitor (optional). Safety: be medically fit to exercise first.

  1. Work in pairs. Student A finds their pulse on the wrist; student B times and records.
  2. A rests for 2 minutes. Then A counts pulses while B times 30 seconds; B records. Repeat twice more.
  3. Calculate A’s average resting pulse rate (pulses per minute).
  4. Repeat after low–moderate exercise (brisk walk, 2 minutes) and moderate–high exercise (jog or run at about a three-quarter pace, 2 minutes).
  5. Swap roles and repeat. Plot the data on a suitable chart or graph.
⚠️ Common mistake: counting for 30 seconds but forgetting that the rate is per minute (double it). The investigation repeats the count three times and averages them.

❓ Quick Quiz

Which of these is NOT one of the three main functions of the circulatory system?

  1. Transporting substances and removing waste
  2. Protecting the body against disease
  3. Helping regulate body temperature
  4. Breaking food down into smaller pieces
Breaking food down is digestion. The circulatory system transports, protects and helps regulate temperature.

Which component of blood is a light yellow liquid, largely water, that carries dissolved oxygen and glucose?

  1. Platelets
  2. Plasma
  3. Red blood cells
  4. White blood cells
Plasma carries dissolved substances (oxygen, glucose), waste (carbon dioxide, urea) and heat. The cells and platelets are solid components.

What is haemoglobin?

  1. An antibody made by white blood cells
  2. A fragment of a larger cell that helps clotting
  3. A red pigment in red blood cells that contains iron and picks up oxygen
  4. The liquid part of blood
Haemoglobin is the iron-containing red pigment in red blood cells that picks up oxygen for transport.

What do white blood cells do?

  1. Protect the body by fighting micro-organisms or producing antibodies
  2. Carry oxygen around the body
  3. Help blood to clot
  4. Carry waste such as urea away from cells
White blood cells fight disease. Oxygen is carried by red blood cells, clotting is by platelets and waste is carried by plasma.

Which blood component helps blood to clot and heal wounds?

  1. Haemoglobin
  2. Platelets
  3. Plasma
  4. Antibodies
Platelets are tiny fragments of larger cells that help blood clot, preventing blood loss and healing wounds.

Why do arteries have thick muscle walls?

  1. To allow oxygen to pass through into cells
  2. To prevent the backflow of blood
  3. To store blood between heartbeats
  4. To withstand the high pressure of blood pumped out of the heart
Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure. Preventing backflow is the job of valves in veins; allowing molecules to pass is the job of thin capillary walls.

Why do veins have valves?

  1. Blood pressure in veins is lower than in arteries, so valves prevent backflow
  2. Blood pressure in veins is very high
  3. To let oxygen pass into surrounding cells
  4. To make the walls thicker
Pressure in veins is lower than in arteries, so valves stop the blood flowing backwards.

Why can oxygen and carbon dioxide pass in and out of capillaries?

  1. Their walls are thick and muscular
  2. They contain valves
  3. Their walls are only one cell thick and have lots of pores
  4. They carry blood away from the heart
Capillary walls are one cell thick with lots of pores, so molecules pass between the blood and the surrounding cells.

Which are the lower chambers of the heart?

  1. The atria
  2. The ventricles
  3. The septa
  4. The venae cavae
Upper chambers = atria; lower chambers = ventricles. The septum is the wall between the left and right sides.

Which statement about the pulmonary artery is correct?

  1. It carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium
  2. It brings deoxygenated blood back to the right atrium
  3. It takes oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the body
  4. It is the only artery that carries deoxygenated blood, taking it to the lungs
The pulmonary artery takes deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. Option 1 is the pulmonary vein, option 2 the vena cava, option 3 the aorta.

What is the pulse?

  1. A wave of vibration through an artery when blood is forced out of the heart
  2. The sound of the valves closing
  3. The flow of blood through the veins
  4. The number of red blood cells per minute
The pulse is the wave through an artery. The “lub-dub” sound is made by the valves closing.

What is the normal heart rate for an adult at rest?

  1. 20–40 beats per minute
  2. 40–60 beats per minute
  3. 60–100 beats per minute
  4. 100–140 beats per minute
The normal adult resting heart rate is between 60 and 100 beats per minute.

1. Explain the role of plasma in the blood. (Summary Q3)
  • Plasma is a light yellow liquid, largely water.
  • It carries dissolved substances such as oxygen and glucose around the body.
  • It carries waste products (carbon dioxide and urea) away from the cells.
  • It transfers heat around the body.
2. Outline two features of each type of blood vessel (artery, vein, capillary) and give one reason arteries have thick walls, veins have valves and capillaries have thin walls. (Summary Q4–5) [6 marks]
  • Artery: carries blood away from the heart; thick muscle walls to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped out of the heart. Usually oxygenated.
  • Vein: carries blood to the heart; thinner walls; valves prevent the backflow of blood because pressure is lower than in arteries. Usually deoxygenated.
  • Capillary: very small, links arteries and veins; walls one cell thick with lots of pores, so molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide can pass in and out of surrounding cells.

7. Explain why the pulse rate is the same as the heart rate. How do you measure the pulse and what is a normal resting rate? (Summary Q6; Experiment 7.1)

The pulse is a wave of vibration that passes through an artery each time blood is forced out of the heart, so there is one pulse for each heartbeat – the pulse rate and heart rate are the same.

Measure: find the pulse at the wrist, count for 30 seconds (with a timer), repeat and average, and convert to beats per minute. A normal adult resting rate is 60–100 beats per minute.
